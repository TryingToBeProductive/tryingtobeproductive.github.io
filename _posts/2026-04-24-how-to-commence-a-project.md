*2026/04/23*

*Estimated Reading Time : 5-6min*

You have defined a clear goal, conducted some research, and outlined a rough timeline. The logical next step is to put that preparation into action. Yet, over-preparation can become a trap: you now understand most of the required steps, but struggle to decide which one to take first.

If your goal is simply to increase X or decrease Y, you can begin by adjusting your time allocation accordingly—spend more time on X and less on Y. For example, you might increase your reading time to read more, or limit internet access to reduce online usage. However, most projects—even personal ones—consist of multiple interdependent components. Learning a new language requires developing reading, listening, writing, and speaking skills, along with vocabulary, grammar, and cultural understanding. Launching a digital product involves building the product, creating a landing page, running beta tests, planning marketing, and deciding on distribution. The list keeps expanding.

There are many possible starting points, but eventually, a choice must be made. At the highest level, this decision often comes down to a structural question: linear vs. parallel execution. A linear approach is intuitive—you complete one stage before moving to the next. A parallel approach, on the other hand, requires a system, such as assigning different tasks to specific days or rotating through focused work cycles.

Most people default to the linear method because it minimizes mental friction and helps sustain momentum. However, when tasks are highly interdependent, a strictly sequential approach can stall progress. The system cannot advance because each component depends on the others. In such cases, a parallel approach becomes necessary, even if it requires more discipline to maintain motivation.

At the next level, you must decide which task to tackle first. Prioritization can be based on three main factors: time, difficulty, and cost.

Some tasks take days, while others take months. If you want all components to finish at roughly the same time, it is logical to start with the longest tasks. On the other hand, if early visible progress is important, completing shorter tasks first can build momentum.

Difficulty introduces another dimension. Harder tasks are more likely to be postponed, which is why advice like “eat the frog first” exists. Nevertheless, our brains are remarkably good at avoiding effort—sometimes by not acting at all. Even when we consciously decide to tackle the hardest task first, our instincts can quietly override that decision. We delay, rationalize, or shift attention, not because we lack understanding, but because immediate comfort often wins over long-term benefit. If this resistance persists even after removing obvious barriers, then relying on intuition alone is no longer sufficient. In such cases, switching to an “easy-first” approach may not be a [compromise](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/), but a necessary strategy to regain momentum.ing more demanding work.

Cost also plays a role. Equipment, materials, and tools can be expensive. While renting or sharing can reduce costs, it rarely eliminates them. When evaluating the cost of each task, it is tempting to assume that larger-scale work always carries greater risk. Following this intuition, one might start with smaller tasks to avoid exceeding the [budget](/2026/03/22/budgeting/). While this approach is safe, it is not always optimal.

Intuition tends to equate scale with cost, but this can be misleading. Instead of avoiding high-stakes tasks entirely, we can reduce their risk by experimenting at a smaller scale. By creating simplified versions or prototypes, we can identify inefficiencies and estimate impact before committing full resources. In this way, rather than focusing only on low-stakes problems, we can strategically engage with high-stakes ones—screening for the most promising solutions while controlling cost.

Taking the first step ultimately requires choosing a path. At the highest level, you decide between linear and parallel execution. At the next level, you prioritize based on time, difficulty, or cost. Together, these dimensions create multiple viable strategies. If you feel overwhelmed by the number of possible approaches, reducing the decision to these core frameworks can provide [clarity](/2026/05/27/clarity/).

Progress begins not with perfect planning, but with a deliberate first step.
