*Estimated Reading Time : 5min*

Nowadays, even the smallest fragments of spare time are targeted by [advertisements](/2026/03/22/recommending-ad-blockers/). Educational platforms such as Duolingo and Brilliant market themselves as superior alternatives to endlessly scrolling through social media. Meanwhile, so-called “time-wasting” platforms continue optimizing their content for maximum accessibility and minimal commitment, attempting to capture even a single minute of our attention. Others advocate for digital detoxes, arguing that we should reclaim our attention by intentionally leaving moments unfilled rather than constantly occupying them with apps and stimulation.

Waiting time is unavoidable. Much of our work depends on coordination with other people, naturally creating gaps between their actions and ours. Even in mostly independent projects, code must compile, packages must be delivered, chemical reactions must finish, and weather conditions must cooperate before harvesting or construction can proceed. Careful planning—whether across months or within a single day—allows us to identify these idle periods ahead of time and prepare for them. The real question is not merely how to eliminate waiting time, but how to use it effectively when elimination is impossible.

At larger timescales, most people juggle multiple projects simultaneously. One strategy is to prioritize tasks with long waiting periods so that smaller tasks can be completed while larger processes continue in the background. Another approach is to aggressively finish short-term work first, allowing preparation for later stages to occur while longer projects process passively. If executed successfully, the former approach may produce the fastest overall completion time, but it requires careful planning to avoid scheduling conflicts. The latter delays the completion of larger projects but provides faster visible progress across the workload as a whole. The trade-off becomes clear: if minimizing total completion time matters most, taking scheduling risks may be worthwhile. If maintaining consistent visible progress is more important, prioritizing shorter tasks becomes the safer strategy.

Conversely, when we already know that tomorrow will contain several short waiting periods—perhaps three to ten minutes at a time—we can decide in advance how those moments should be used. Small tasks such as reviewing notes, memorizing flashcards, brainstorming [ideas](/2026/05/20/ideas/), or organizing reminders can often fit comfortably into these windows. Waiting periods can also be used for mental simulation: rehearsing the next steps of a process to improve execution speed once work resumes. Or, as advocates of mindfulness would suggest, we may choose to do nothing at all and simply remain present in the moment.

The most important factor determining how spare moments should be used is the cost of context switching. One reason multitasking often reduces both speed and quality is that the brain requires time to reload relevant information and rebuild focus. Even tasks that last only a few minutes involve workflows, pitfalls, and quality standards that must be mentally reconstructed. As a result, filling every small gap with unrelated work may increase the number of completed tasks while simultaneously slowing the resumption of larger projects.

Because of this, rules for using waiting time should account not only for immediate productivity, but also for their effect on subsequent work. If the primary task is highly time-sensitive, filling spare moments with unrelated activities may become counterproductive. In such situations, mental preparation for rapid re-entry into the main workflow may be the more effective choice. On the other hand, if delaying the larger project carries little cost, using spare time for secondary tasks becomes much less harmful.

Aggressively utilizing every moment may sound productive, but it introduces [trade-offs](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/). At larger scales, tightly packed schedules reduce flexibility. At smaller scales, excessive task-switching delays the completion of primary work. To achieve the level of performance we desire, we must evaluate not only how much work is being done, but also the hidden costs associated with how it is done. Productivity is ultimately the management of trade-offs, so even spare moments should be analyzed before enacted.
