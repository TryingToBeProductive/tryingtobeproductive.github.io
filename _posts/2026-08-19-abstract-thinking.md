A chapter of a given math textbook usually follows the same order: the definition of a theorem, its proof, and examples as exercises. This is a typical progression from the abstract to the concrete. Most of the time, students begin to understand the concepts after going through the examples rather than through abstract proofs and definitions.

Even when people talk about productivity hacks or tools, they share specific products, workflows, or tips that have very limited use cases. This is understandable because, similar to mathematics, our brains cannot process words or symbols alone. They need physical representations to anchor ideas in our minds so that they can be understood and used.

Nevertheless, turning concrete knowledge into abstractions significantly expands the range of its applications without requiring further study. Concepts such as systems theory, technical debt, activation energy, statistics, evolutionary bugs, [knowledge management](/2026/05/13/organizing-notes/), and so much more can be used time and time again when building workflows, priority lists, and [routines](/2026/02/20/routines/); choosing hardware and software; or reevaluating one's thought processes and future plans. As you can see from the previous sentence, the possible transfer examples are almost endless.

What makes abstraction so powerful is that it has something analogy doesn't: flexibility. Whenever we try to use an analogy to describe different topics, it tends to break down under even a little bit of scrutiny. Abstracted ideas, on the other hand, have already shed many of their [details](/2026/03/06/details/), allowing us to mold them to fit almost any situation. In an extreme case, one can even misuse an abstraction, as long as it helps the user solve a problem.

Our brains hate abstract thinking because it requires us to think beyond concrete examples. Yet, once we have abstracted our knowledge or experiences, they can be transferred to many kinds of situations. In addition, the conditions for their use are almost nonexistent, which further speeds up the adoption process. Resources, especially time and mental energy, will always be limited. To efficiently expand and improve our productivity systems, recycling what we already have is the cheapest and fastest approach.
