Whether your life demands it or the environment forces it upon you, you have to spend some time adjusting your schedules, workflows, or routines to keep up with changing circumstances. On an individual level, transition periods can occur when you are moving on to a new project, experiencing a personal paradigm shift that makes you feel alienated from your current circumstances, or changing careers. On a larger scale, they can range from cultural shifts and emerging generations to norm-breaking innovations. Everyone knows that adaptation takes time, but that does not make the process any easier to go through.

Knowing when to initiate change is easy because you can feel the pressure. No matter how rigid or effective your routines and systems may be, significant adjustments become necessary when you experience persistent irritation, whether because you are constantly failing to meet other goals in your life or because the urgency and importance of a given problem have changed dramatically. The real resistance comes from the obvious downsides of “killing the momentum.” If you actually left a memo for yourself before starting your routines or workflow, you would be aware that there is a chance of your old bad habits or problems returning. Nevertheless, due to time constraints or declining relevance, continuing to do what has been working may no longer be sustainable.

Another obstacle that needs to be addressed before committing to change, especially when dealing with events outside of your control, is the possibility of the workload doubling. The annoying part about societal shifts is that they happen gradually. As a result, during the transition period, you somehow have to satisfy both the previous state and the future state at the same time. You might be able to conjure up examples such as dealing with two different generations, each with either a conventional or rebellious attitude, or being expected to remain authentic while also moving quickly in the age of AI.

Fortunately, tackling these two problems not only removes barriers to action but also helps us plan better for the upcoming interim periods.

Preventing yourself from reverting to your worst behaviors or encountering the same old troubles will not be easy when there are no guardrails in place. Therefore, when building a new system for the next chapter of your life, focusing not only on the tasks that lie ahead but also on the old problems that may resurface once their existing safeguards are removed will determine the success of your adjustments. Of course, this will mean more thinking and more testing, but a transition period is usually much shorter than a stable period, which makes the return on investment high.

Trying to meet opposing demands will not be easy if you do not have sufficient resources. As such, if you are willing to tackle the challenge head-on, be sure to have enough time, energy, and finances for your endeavor. If you cannot guarantee that, using the adjustment period to master each demand individually may be the better option. When a workflow becomes second nature, it frees up time and energy to practice and experiment with new workflows designed for different objectives. It is called a “period” for a reason: you cannot transition immediately, and there is no single correct process for doing so.

There will always come a time when we have to reconsider our conventional approaches and make changes that fit both our inner and outer lives. Still, simply shaking things up would not only produce mediocre results; it could also allow past complications to reemerge. Performance improves by acquiring new skills and tools while eliminating issues and failure points. How you spend your transition time will affect your future productivity, so use that time to think through and plan the adjustments needed to build better systems, routines, and workflows that can satisfy both your past and future demands.
