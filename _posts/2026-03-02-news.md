*Estimated Reading Time : 4-5min*

Most people have been told to “follow the news to be an informed citizen.” Before the rise of social media, you could agree with that statement to some degree. However, once people realized that news — especially extreme or sensational stories — is a powerful way to capture attention, it increasingly became a tool for generating traffic rather than informing the public.

Those who create or influence the news — politicians, PR managers, activists, and others — have also shifted their focus in many cases from educating the public to promoting themselves. Meanwhile, organizations that genuinely aim to reduce suffering, celebrate human progress, or promote intellectual growth are finding it harder to reach the people who could actually take meaningful action.

But if we think about it more carefully, do we really need to give so much of our attention to the news?

Unless your job is to curate or analyze news, most of the information we consume flows out as quickly as it flows in. Even when it stirs strong emotions, there is often little we can realistically do about it beyond continuing to watch, scroll, or participate in discussions that lead nowhere. And when we intentionally step away from media, the truly major or personally important stories still manage to reach us.

When reconsidering our media diet, the concept of “locus of control” can serve as a guide. Psychologist Julian Rotter introduced this idea to describe how people perceive control over events in their lives. There are areas where we have expertise or can contribute meaningfully. There are also areas where, no matter how much we care, we have little to no influence.

When confronted with issues outside your control, you have two options: selectively ignore them or expand your range of control to address them. In the case of most news events — especially those happening in distant places — expanding your capabilities to meaningfully intervene is often unrealistic.

By understanding which parts of reality you can influence and which you cannot, you can filter what information deserves your attention. Instead of passively consuming everything, focus on knowledge that you can actually apply to create positive change in your own life and community.

News consumption has, for many people, become another form of doomscrolling. If it does not expand your agency or improve your actions, it may simply be draining your time and energy. Step back, regain control of your attention, and direct it toward areas where you can truly make an impact. And don't worry about missing out, because you won't and big news will still break into your bubble anyways.
