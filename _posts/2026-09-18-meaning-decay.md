Usually, we are busy and distracted enough to prevent us from sitting still and contemplating our own existence. However, if your productivity system is working too well, you might find yourself in an unoccupied situation, both mentally and physically, wandering into existential thoughts.

It is hard to argue against the importance of meaning, since typical [goal-setting](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/) is simply another way to add meaning to your work. However, if you zoom far enough into the future, most matter and energy will eventually become dispersed and reshuffled, erasing most of the information that existed before. The easiest example to imagine is the Sun engulfing planet Earth, burning everything we valued in our lifetime. The extreme version would be heat death, where even atoms and fundamental particles can no longer maintain their structures due to the ever-expanding universe.

Of course, existential crises are nothing new, and many philosophers have thought of countless ways to manage them. One famous framework would be absurdism by Albert Camus, which states that when confronted with the absurd reality that a person is looking for meaning in a universe indifferent to meaning, rather than trying to escape it, we should embrace it by finding meaning despite knowing its absurdity—which is itself an absurd act. Or there is the much simpler concept of "optimistic nihilism": if every mistake you made won't matter in the far future anyway, why not take on challenges and make meaning as you wish? There is also a useful tool: logical breakdown. If any action is devoid of meaning, wouldn't that mean that reacting to the void is also meaningless?

To some extent, these concepts do handle the idea of life very well. Nevertheless, it is hard to ignore the fact that they de-emphasize the role of consequences and rewards in our actions. For example, knowing that pain is temporary or that punishments won't be remembered doesn't change the fact that we fear them. On the other hand, even if we recognize that any success, joy, or legacy created in our lifetime will hardly remain, we still seek them.

To bring back the importance of outcomes, we should pay attention to the lifespan of meaning.

For the sake of the argument, let's pretend that every action, piece of work, piece of content, or even simple object could be abstracted as "information." And any information also carries its subjective "meaning." In this context, meaning contains not just a description but also the degree to which a person values it.

For kids, "playing with their toys" gives them a high amount of "meaning" through the joy the activity provides, but it gradually decreases as they get bored or grow out of it altogether. Students find "meaning" in studying by getting higher grades or acquiring new knowledge, and slowly lose it when their scores plateau or the material becomes less intuitive. Adults are willing to invest significant amounts of time to meet their deadlines, bring in more sales, enjoy hobbies, and hone their skills. Yet when a project has been terminated, wages stop increasing, everything there is to experience has been experienced, or improvement stalls, their passion wanes alongside it.

To phrase it mathematically, we can think of each piece of information, work, hobby, or project as having a changing amount of "meaning" over time. For each (*n*)-th piece of information, its meaning can be expressed as a function

$y_{n}(t) = f_{n}(t)$

Each also has its own lifespan, during which it remains relevant:

$t_{n,{start}} \leq t \leq t_{n,{end}}$  

Since we usually have multiple sources of meaning at any given time, the total amount of "meaning" at a specific point in time can be approximated as

<div>
\[ Y(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(t) \qquad(t_{n,\mathrm{start}}\leq t\leq t_{n,\mathrm{end}}) \]
</div>

This gives us a rough model of our current "meaningfulness." If we let *t* change dynamically, we can also visualize how it changes over time. More importantly, we can imagine what happens if we stop adding new sources of meaning. As the existing *f_n(t)*s gradually decline, the total *Y(t)* would eventually approach zero.

You might already see and have experienced the pattern. Initially, "information" was filled with "meaning." As time goes on, the amount of "meaning" might peak at a certain point in time, such as on the day of a performance or close to a [deadline](/2026/04/01/deadlines/), but generally, it gets drained gradually. Finally, at some point, "the information" becomes empty of "meaning," either due to our state of being or entropy. You might find some similarity with "half-life" as found in physics and chemistry. Of course, it doesn't neatly fit the formula

<div>
\[ N(t) = N_0\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{t_{1/2}}} \]
</div>

Still, it describes a phenomenon in which a major characteristic of a given material decays over time.

Just as pharmacists routinely administer drugs to stabilize their concentration, or radioactive isotopes get replaced at nuclear power plants for continuous operation, we can keep our overall "meaning" high by refilling it with valuable "information" periodically. Some of them might come whether we like it or not: deadlines or changes in societal expectations. While others require us to actively incorporate them: learning or trying new hobbies.

Even when everything will eventually return to dust, as long as your actions and thoughts are driven by potential outcomes, you are still riding the wave of "meaning." This also means that if you don't feel the push, both from outside and within, it indicates the need to collect enough work, passion, and projects to reform that wave.

As long as we exist, thinking about our own significance to the greater universe is unavoidable. Rather than letting the cosmological scale deter us, use the contrast to highlight the subjective value of our daily lives. Our tasks matter a lot, even to the dismay of our comfort. So don't stop improving your productivity system, because it will help you experience the high of meaning even higher.
