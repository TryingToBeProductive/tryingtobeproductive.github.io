If you know Warren Buffett, you may have heard about his favorite book, *The Intelligent Investor*. Looking at the title alone, one might believe that it teaches smart financial strategies. However, most of the advice in the book can be boiled down to “don’t make stupid investments.” It could be as simple as “buy low, sell high,” “don’t take risks you can’t afford,” or even as mundane as “have an emergency fund before trading stocks.” It was quite an eye-opener for me because it taught me that being smart doesn’t mean operating in a sophisticated manner, but rather being less dumb.

Well, now we know how to be “intelligent,” so let’s ask how to build systems that reduce our [foolish](/2026/05/17/silliness/) behavior. Our brains are not perfect, which becomes a problem when we try to be “rational.” Not only do we have incomplete models of how the world works, but our minds are also very good at creating logical chains where there shouldn’t be any. Going through as many mental simulations as possible can cover a lot of ground, but not all of it. As such, we also need critical feedback early on to course-correct before we lose everything.

The first pillar of our structure is absorbing existing knowledge. The cost of learning through experience is too high compared to being told what is senseless from the beginning. Of course, there is a limit to what objective facts can offer. However, if there are pitfalls that can be avoided through knowledge, we can pay fewer “life lessons” to become “smart.”

The second pillar is self-assessment. Nothing works in one go, but if something keeps failing or showing errors, then something needs to be fixed. Even if we can’t control every variable, most would agree that we can’t blame all our problems on the environment. To identify issues and prepare before things go wrong, personal reviews or daily feedback can be useful tools. It could be as simple as asking, “What are three things that need improvement, and how?” every day. When you spend a few minutes reflecting and planning improvements, those small frictions in your life start to erode, leaving behind a smoother path toward your goals.

The final pillar is searching. Easily avoidable traps aren’t any less dangerous if you don’t know where they are. There is always something that could go wrong, and spotting it beforehand can save countless hours and mental energy. Luckily, if you have already researched the area well and improved what you can, it is natural that you would develop some educated guesses—and an incentive to look for potential sinkholes and fix them. You don’t want to put all that effort to waste or interrupt your [gradual](/2026/03/25/incremental-progress/) progress. Looking out for these risks not only makes your work more meaningful, but also prevents your projects from collapsing unexpectedly.

Having higher intelligence could also mean having lower stupidity. Therefore, making fewer dumb choices can be a shortcut to a smarter life. Through learning, assessing, and searching, we can remove obvious roadblocks, identify and fix flaws, and reinforce structures to withstand predictable [failures](/2026/08/18/learning-from-failures/). Sometimes, improving performance doesn’t require fancy equipment—it just requires removing the obvious barriers that slow us down.
