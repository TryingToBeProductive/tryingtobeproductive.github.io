When it comes to the power of incremental progress, physical training provides one of the clearest examples. Let’s say you want to do 100 push-ups without stopping. Most people would find this impossible at first, because their bodies simply can’t handle it. However, if you shift your [goal](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/) to increasing your push-up count by just one each day until you reach 100, the task becomes far more achievable. This approach also significantly reduces the daily workload while allowing your muscles to adapt gradually. Even as it becomes more difficult near the end, the barrier remains low enough to overcome. The idea is simple: small changes accumulate into meaningful results—much like the logic behind building routines.

However, there is another advantage to prioritizing gradual improvement: it allows for the emergence of complexity. If you are interested in complex systems, you may have come across the idea of “self-organization.” When you examine systems closely—from physical and biological phenomena to social structures—you will find countless smaller interactions, feedback loops, and parameters that seem impossible to design all at once. Yet, together, they create order and can even adapt, self-heal, or replicate. Building such systems from scratch is nearly impossible. But, much like in nature, when variables are introduced gradually, feedback loops are added carefully, connections are formed intentionally, and the flow of effort and resources is adjusted proportionally to the system’s growth, complex and sustainable systems begin to emerge. While they may not reach the scale or sophistication of natural systems, they can still be powerful tools for improving performance and achieving long-term goals.

That said, incremental progress has its limitations. Time-sensitive projects often cannot afford slow, step-by-step development. You also need to recognize that the environment around you is constantly changing because human progress never stops. If your work depends on evolving technologies, services, or even geopolitical conditions, your system may become obsolete before it is fully developed.

Another drawback is the difficulty of responding to unexpected outcomes. This is where the idea of "emergence" comes in—the point at which simple components interact to produce outcomes that cannot be predicted from the parts alone. As you build a system gradually, it may develop properties you did not anticipate. Some of these side effects can be beneficial—for example, a [note-taking](/2026/02/11/note-taking/) system evolving into a creativity engine, or an efficient sharing process enabling a team to reach a state of flow. Others can be harmful, such as [communication](/2026/03/08/notifications/) tools reducing spontaneous conversations that spark ideas, or an overemphasis on metrics at the expense of meaningful work. The challenge is that it is nearly impossible to predict how a system will behave once it reaches a certain level of complexity. The best approach is to continuously reassess whether your system remains aligned with your goals by tracking outcomes and reflecting on your results.

Unimaginable tasks become achievable when broken down into small steps. Whether you are developing a skill or building a system to support your productivity, progress is possible when time is on your side. However, when speed and adaptability are critical, incremental approaches may not be suitable. Like any tool, this mindset has its strengths and limitations. Understanding both allows you to use it effectively—improving performance without overextending yourself.
