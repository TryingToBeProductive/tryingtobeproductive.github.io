*Estimated Reading Time : 4-5min*

We are all painfully aware that health is a prerequisite for productivity. No matter how much willpower or motivation we have, if the body refuses to move, nothing gets done. Over the long term, prioritizing physical well-being pays dividends: the longer your healthspan, the more projects—both personal and social—you can engage in, satisfying your ambitions while improving your mental well-being along the way.

If you look through the literature, you will find plenty of cutting-edge research on drugs, devices, and routines that promise longer life. However, as Why We Die: The New Science of Aging and the Quest for Immortality by Venki Ramakrishnan points out, the most reliable interventions remain the basics: a balanced diet, regular [exercise](/2026/02/23/exercise/), and sufficient sleep. For this reason, the rest of this discussion will focus on these three pillars.

Although there is nothing sophisticated about eating well, staying active, and getting a good night’s [rest](/2026/02/26/taking-breaks/), most of us struggle to maintain them consistently. We understand that our performance depends on our physical condition, but sustaining it creates conflicts between time, money, and mental effort when poorly managed.

A rough calculation makes this clear: meal preparation and eating take 2–3 hours, intense exercise 30–60 minutes, and sleep 7–9 hours. That amounts to roughly 9.5–14 hours per day devoted to maintaining health, leaving only 10–14.5 hours for work and other pursuits. This is a significant commitment. Moreover, once we become accustomed to a healthy routine, it can be difficult to deviate from it. Our microbiome may become less tolerant of unhealthy food, we may feel restless when skipping exercise, and our bodies may continue to wake at the same time even without an alarm. While this self-sustaining nature sounds appealing, it comes at the cost of flexibility. Since we cannot stretch time to recover what is lost, the best option is to manage it more deliberately. By [tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/) how you spend your time and adjusting your schedule, you can identify patterns that maximize efficiency. We may not achieve perfect balance, but we can reduce the trade-offs.

Biology can also shape society in ways that work against us. In the past, high-fat and high-sugar foods were scarce, energy conservation was essential, and those who minimized unnecessary effort often had an advantage. As a result, we evolved to seek calorie-dense foods, conserve energy, and override sleepiness when necessary. Today, modern systems and supply chains cater to these instincts at low cost—through fast food, sedentary work, cars, caffeine, and blue light exposure. Meanwhile, what is considered “healthy” often becomes more expensive because it has not been prioritized at scale. This creates another layer of [conflict](/2026/09/13/system-conflict/). One way to respond is to reduce spending on non-essential stimulants such as energy drinks, cutting cost on exercise by canceling gym membership and not buying equipments, and redirecting those resources toward higher-quality food and sleep environments, improving well-being without increasing overall cost.

The final conflict is more philosophical: autonomy. Maintaining healthy habits often requires rejecting behaviors we consciously recognize as harmful, even when they provide short-term pleasure. At times, this can feel restrictive, as if we are denied the freedom to choose what we enjoy. But were we ever fully free to begin with? Debates around free will and determinism remain unresolved, but consider the deterministic perspective for a moment. Many industries are designed to shape our behavior. The food industry optimizes products for addictiveness rather than nutrition. The entertainment industry invests billions in capturing attention, pulling time and energy away from physically demanding activities like exercise and toward passive consumption. Technology companies design highly stimulating platforms to maximize engagement, often at the expense of sleep and focus.

By [framing](/2026/05/22/framing/) our desires as something shaped or planted by external forces, it becomes easier—or at least more motivating—to question them. Even if our choices are not entirely free, some influences are still better than others. In that sense, following what our bodies signal—rest when tired, move when restless, eat when genuinely hungry—may still lead to better outcomes than following impulses engineered for profit. At the very least, unlike profit-driven corporations, our bodies are generally aligned with our long-term well-being.

Maintaining a healthy routine directly improves daily performance. However, sustaining it requires addressing the underlying conflicts of time, money, and autonomy. When managed well, health and productivity reinforce each other, forming a positive feedback loop that steadily enhances both over time.
