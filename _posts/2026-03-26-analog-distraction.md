You may have heard people from previous generations pushing back against even earlier generations who claimed that young people’s brains were being “rotted” by new forms of media. For example, magazines were once criticized in much the same way that smartphones are today. At first glance, this argument seems valid, but it may not feel directly applicable to our current situation, especially when the internet appears to consume such a large portion of our time. However, once we manage to [block](/2026/02/24/blocking-distractions/), filter, and effectively control modern digital distractions, we may be surprised to find that those older claims still hold true. We do not need digital [tools](/2026/04/27/tools/) to procrastinate—analog ones can serve the same purpose just as effectively.

When we are asked to think about the kinds of entertainment our parents or grandparents had, television, radio, newspapers, and magazines usually come to mind. Distractions that require subscriptions are relatively easy to manage, because if you do not pay for the service or delivery, there is simply no material to consume. Radio is more difficult to restrict, since it is often free to access and can even be critical for survival in a disaster zone. One possible approach is to disable easy access on your devices and store emergency radio equipment in a dedicated, less accessible location. This way, it remains available when needed, but is less likely to be used as a convenient escape from work.

Gossip and long phone calls—especially when actively engaged in—can also disrupt productivity. Because we are social creatures, these activities tap deeply into our psychological reward systems, making them highly appealing. Of course, cutting off relationships entirely is neither practical nor desirable. Instead, we can allocate specific time blocks for social interaction in advance, making spontaneous engagement less likely. By [managing and tracking this time](/2026/02/09/time-management/), we can find a balance where socializing remains meaningful without becoming a persistent distraction.

One of the most surprising analog distractions is books—not only comics, but also fiction and even non-fiction. Much of modern work involves reading, from spreadsheets to legal documents. While books are also part of this broader activity, they are often perceived as more engaging than work-related [reading](/2026/02/27/reading/). The difference lies in their relative “dryness.” Our brains are highly imaginative—given a book, they can construct entire worlds from simple text. In contrast, real-life projects do not have the luxury of indulging in fantasy. As a result, they often feel dull and less engaging, making it harder to stay focused. Still, most books are physical objects. If they are not easily accessible, and if we avoid environments such as libraries or bookstores, they are less likely to become tools for procrastination. On the other hand, if you are more of a ebook person, simply not downloading the books beforehand would do the trick.

Physical activities—such as sports, running excess errands, or exploring the city—can also function as distractions. Due to their energy-demanding nature, they are less likely to replace low-effort tasks. However, when a task requires significant mental energy, physical activities can become appealing alternatives. Because it is difficult to directly compare mental and physical energy expenditure, the brain may prioritize physical activity under the assumption that it will conserve more energy. To counter this tendency, we can increase the friction required to leave our workspace—for example, by adding preparation steps or small barriers like locks. Once the perceived effort outweighs the appeal, it becomes easier to remain focused on the task at hand.

Long before the digital era, humans developed technologies such as the printing press and wireless communication to capture and direct attention. Our ability to distract ourselves is not new. Fortunately, the same principles we use to manage digital distractions can also be applied to analog ones: limiting access, increasing friction, and structuring their use. The challenge of maintaining focus is ongoing, so remaining aware of both our environment and our behavior is essential for sustaining performance over time.
