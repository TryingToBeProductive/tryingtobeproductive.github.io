*2026/08/23*

*Estimated Reading Time : 3-4min*

We have all heard stories of companies going bankrupt because their managers weren't able to get rid of the sunk-cost fallacy. When a project becomes an obvious failure, further investment is futile. Recoup the losses through new projects and initiatives, not by continuing to invest in a failing scheme.

Even though the stakes are much lower, we also have to be able to call it quits when one of our multi-month projects is barely working. Since time, energy, and cash are severely limited for an individual or a group endeavor, cutting down on waste is crucial for juggling multiple life objectives. While at the same time, giving up too early is one of the many reasons why some people fail to realize their [dreams](/2026/08/25/expectation/).

To make a convincing argument to ourselves that things need to change and that we have to act accordingly, we can ask, "If I had to start over given what I know now, would I do it again?" If the answer is a strong NO, you can safely say that stopping is the best way forward. However, if you are unsure, it may still be worth further investment. Any skill, project, or proposal requires a maturation period, and the middle period will always be the most boring and least rewarding part. To prevent ourselves from succumbing to the dread, we can make it a rule to quit only when we are absolutely certain that something is not working.

For tasks that take days or months to finish, they give us multiple points at which we can course-correct. On the other hand, a task that should have been done in a day doesn't allow us much time to plan and execute a reevaluation. Especially when you are near the end of the day and have spent too much time procrastinating, it is tempting to sacrifice sleep and other [routines](/2026/02/20/routines/) to do the work you promised yourself you would finish that day. In this case, following your gut mostly results in a half-baked outcome and a sleep-deprived tomorrow in which you resent the decision you made the night before.

The sunk cost in this situation is the time you spent not doing your work, and counterintuitively, keeping it that way can actually help you recoup your losses the next day without affecting your entire day's performance. When you can't change your actions in time, change the time itself to give yourself the chance to do so.

Persuading anyone, even ourselves, to quit is difficult, no matter the scale of the work. Nevertheless, the scale can become the deciding factor in controlling the damage already done. When a project has been running for a long period of time, completely losing faith means termination. Yet, if it is a task that could be done within 24 hours, waiting for the day to change is the least costly option. Productivity systems should always have a suspension [protocol](/2026/04/16/templates/). Make one yourself with several potential situations in mind. Knowing how to stop can sometimes be more important than knowing when.
