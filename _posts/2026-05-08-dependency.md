*Estimated Reading Time : 4-5min*

We like to think of ourselves as independent—capable of living our lives and accomplishing tasks without relying on others. However, even a brief moment of self-reflection quickly dismantles that image. We depend on income, social safety nets, medicine, public transportation, trust, and global supply chains simply to survive. Programmers understand this well when an application suddenly refuses to work because a dependency version is either too old or too new. Even on a smaller scale, we rely on pre-built knowledge, services, and frameworks to construct our workflows. Productivity systems do not exist in a vacuum, so analyzing their dependencies can become the starting point for improvement.

Given enough time, we should be able to map out the tools and systems our workflows rely on. Some dependencies are obvious: a computer that, if broken, halts our work entirely; a gym that, if closed, reduces the intensity of our workouts; or grocery stores that provide the fuel for our bodies. Others remain visible but underappreciated, such as electricity powering our devices, SaaS platforms automating [repetitive](/2026/04/06/automation/) tasks, or delivery services supplying necessary items. Finally, there are the nearly invisible foundations of productivity itself: willpower, health, and mental stability.

Once we begin listing our dependencies, we may also realize that some are less essential than we originally believed. Not all work requires constant internet access. Multiple productivity apps may be replaceable by a single system. Restaurants and pre-made meals may be conveniences rather than necessities. If we want to reduce reliance on external structures, removing unnecessary attachments is the first step. Doing so also frees time, money, and attention, which can then be redirected toward tools and systems that contribute more directly to meaningful outcomes.

The next step is building backups against dependency failure. Sometimes this is literal, such as storing files in the cloud so work can continue on another device, or maintaining an emergency fund in case of unemployment. Other times, it requires developing alternatives: lightweight scripts that recreate the essential functions of existing software, workout routines that require no equipment, or familiarity with multiple stores that provide similar products. Beyond safeguarding workflows, searching for alternatives also creates opportunities for improvement. Sometimes, a backup may become the superior option.

No matter how creative we become, some dependencies remain irreplaceable. In these cases, the best approach is to clarify our locus of control and build routines around what we can actually manage. Most of us cannot reshape social infrastructure, but we can influence the non-negotiable foundations of our performance: willpower, health, and mental [calmness](/2026/04/16/calm/). These can be preserved through structured schedules, deliberate planning, and regular reflection. Treating personal routines with the same rigidity as public infrastructure prevents our internal systems from collapsing under instability.

To gain greater control over our performance, we must identify and optimize the dependencies within our lives. By removing unnecessary burdens, making essential systems more resilient, and strengthening what cannot be replaced, we improve productivity while conserving both physical and mental energy. Dependencies make performance possible; understanding them allows us to build workflows that are both resilient and effective.
