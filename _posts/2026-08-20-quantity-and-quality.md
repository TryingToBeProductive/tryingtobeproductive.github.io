An age-old debate: quantity vs. quality. On the pro-quality side, there are phrases such as "Work smarter, not harder," "Less is more," and the literal "Quality over quantity." On the pro-quantity side, there are quotes like "Genius is 1% inspiration, 99% perspiration," "Throw enough mud at the wall and some will stick," and "Outwork everyone."

Even under the same domain and leadership, a given strategy can produce opposite results. During WWII, the Soviets used overwhelming numbers—troops, tanks, artillery, and planes—to push back the Germans and eventually capture their capital. There is even the famous quote, "Quantity has a quality all its own," praising such a method. However, during the Cold War, their submarine fleet was in a dire situation. The Soviets had the numbers, of course, but they did not prioritize stealth technology—that is, quality. As a result, their overwhelming fleet of submarines became sitting ducks, ready to be taken down one by one without being able to fire back at even a single enemy.

Looking closely at the quotes, demands, and historical accounts, at the end of the day, people want "quality" results. To achieve them, they sometimes use precision control (through meticulous planning and preparation which will always be important), while at other times they rely on brute force. We already agree that the goal is quality; the interesting question is whether quality should be pursued through precision or through quantity.

As a process for reaching quality, quantity is extremely powerful. With enough iteration cycles, testing, and prototype generation, a better solution will eventually emerge. When combined with mass production, tiny improvements result in big returns. A typical example would be Toyota's Kaizen philosophy. Every worker on the assembly line can contribute ideas to make both the process and the product better. When the same enhancements are applied to millions of cars, you get a higher-quality product line.

Even when we are acquiring a new skill or honing an existing one, large numbers are hard to beat. When we are starting out, most people have no idea where to begin, what to prioritize, or how to learn. As such, we hit the books, follow tutorials, or simply try anything until we find a working style that we can repeat. To reach an advanced level, most advice tends to boil down to "practice, practice, practice." This holds true because many [blind spots](/2026/05/04/blind-spots/) become visible only when the rest is crystal clear. No matter how many times I have done the same [routine](/2026/02/20/routines/), I am always surprised by the discoveries I still make each day.

With all its strength, the numbers game still has to deal with the immovable wall of resource limitations. There are two approaches: comply or break. Modifying productivity systems to obey the restrictions and reach higher-quality performance is a classic "Necessity is the mother of invention" mindset. Modifying the constraints themselves is typical systems thinking. Either way, if you feel friction when trying to use quantity to bear quality, resource management may be the real problem.

We all strive for quality, but that does not mean we should reach it through quality means. With sufficient resources, quantity can help increase quality through statistical power and compounding effort. Performance is the result, but the systems are where it is built. When precision is hard to come by, forcing your way through can keep you productive.
