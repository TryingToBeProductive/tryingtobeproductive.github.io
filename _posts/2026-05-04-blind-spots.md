*Estimated Reading Time : 4-5min*

No matter how many times we encounter them, blind spots still catch us off guard. What makes them frustrating is not that we *cannot* see them, but that we fail to notice them—even when they sit right under our noses. We can offer excuses: lack of knowledge, limited hands-on experience, flawed assumptions, cognitive biases, or simply that something had never been an issue before. Still, letting things slip through unnoticed and dealing with the consequences afterward is inefficient. Most would agree that preventing problems in advance saves far more time than fixing them later.

One of the simplest ways to detect blind spots is to read widely. This could mean books within your field or works on psychology that explore common human biases. Having these pitfalls pointed out early can help you avoid well-known mistakes. Authors were once beginners themselves, so they tend to emphasize even basic pitfalls for inexperienced readers. Of course, some ideas will go over your head at first. Even so, a vague awareness helps—you may recognize their importance midway through a task and adjust your approach before it is too late. [Taking notes](/2026/02/11/note-taking/) on ideas that *feel* important, even before fully understanding them, can accelerate learning by making them easier to revisit later.

A more direct approach is to imitate experts. This gives you a clear baseline for comparison. When your workflow deviates, identify the differences and correct them until the gap disappears. You do not need to understand every step immediately. In fact, the more counterintuitive a step seems, the more likely it exists to prevent a specific problem rather than to improve efficiency. If your goal is to avoid those problems entirely, following expert processes faithfully is often enough.

If you can receive feedback from experts, the process becomes even faster. Professionals may perform complex tasks instinctively, but when observing others, they can quickly sense when something is off—and explain why, drawing on accumulated experience. This saves you from the time-consuming process of identifying errors on your own and instead gives you access to carefully curated corrections.

However, some tasks are too unconventional to have reliable guides, or too detailed to justify thorough documentation. In such cases, trial and error can become costly. A better approach is to carefully record your process from the beginning. Document everything—from major steps to subtle movements. This forces you to confront assumptions and notice overlooked [details](/2026/03/06/details/). The result may be a long and messy wall of text, but that is fine. The goal is not to create a polished manual, but to have a thinking ground. If done digitally, you can refine and expand specific parts of the step over time without rewriting everything.

This approach resembles the Feynman technique, where you identify gaps in understanding by teaching a concept simply. However, even though it is powerful, such technique requires effort beyond our objective. Your [goal](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/) is not to teach others, but to clarify your thinking enough to guide your own actions.

Ironically, blind spots are hardest to notice when others have already solved the problems for you. Effective managers create smooth workplaces, social systems prevent instability, and software blocks malicious attacks. Because these protections work quietly, it is easy to assume they are unnecessary. The danger is that we may remove them in pursuit of change, not realizing what they were preventing.

Fortunately, these errors tend to occur under specific conditions—most often when we remove or replace something—allowing us to focus our prevention efforts where they matter most. Before getting rid of managers, social programs, or protective systems, ask yourself: *How bad would things have been if they hadn’t been there?* Unexpectedly, gratitude is not just a moral virtue—it is also a practical tool for uncovering blind spots that others have already worked to hide.

Avoiding blind spots requires a combination of knowledge acquisition, imitation, self-interrogation, and awareness of unseen protections. Preventive measures demand time and cognitive effort, and their benefits are often invisible. Still, if mistakes are what slow us down, then investing in prevention is not optional—it is essential for our productivity.
