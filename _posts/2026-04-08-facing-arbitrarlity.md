Most reasonable economists would suggest that inflation should be kept around 2%. An average citizen might assume that this figure was derived from rigorous analysis. In reality, it originated from an impulsive statement made by a New Zealand's finance minister. Although there were negotiations afterward, the number was not grounded in clear theoretical reasoning. Yet this seemingly arbitrary decision helped stabilize the economy, prompting other nations to adopt similar targets. It may be logically unsatisfying, but it has produced workable results, enabling participants in the market to operate more effectively.

In productivity discourse, it is often emphasized that we should clarify our thoughts, processes, and goals in order to better control our actions and maximize performance. Many tools—such as [task managers](/2026/02/22/to-do-list/), [time tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/), and [note-taking](/2026/02/11/note-taking/) systems—are recommended on this basis. Clearing mental fog is indeed one of the most effective ways to improve focus. However, how much ambiguity we should eliminate is rarely discussed.

You may encounter this question firsthand when you begin [time tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/) or hyper-scheduling. For instance, you could group dishwashing, dusting, grocery shopping, and showering under the single category of “household chores.” Alternatively, you could track how many minutes you spend sitting on the toilet, creating an extremely precise record of your day while simultaneously increasing the friction of tracking itself. Even when planning your schedule, you might divide your time into blocks of 30 minutes or one hour. But if you think for few seconds, 17 minutes or 1 hour and 7 minutes are equally valid units of time and may better reflect how long tasks actually take. The trade-off is that such precision increases the cognitive effort required to coordinate your schedule.

Humans are remarkably good at subcategorizing. Given enough attention, any domain can become a world of its own. However, clearer boundaries also introduce more variables to manage, more mental models to maintain, and greater memory demands. Unless your work explicitly requires such granularity, excessive division and over-clarification can reduce performance.

Ultimately, [clarity](/2026/05/27/clarity/) and arbitrariness are both tools that should serve our broader goals, not ends in themselves. Crystallizing our thoughts helps us identify [priorities](/2026/03/05/priorities/) and make rational decisions. At the same time, a degree of intentional ambiguity can prevent us from getting lost in unnecessary detail and failing to complete meaningful work. Regardless of the process we adopt, it must produce tangible results. Therefore, ambiguity should not be eliminated, but deliberately incorporated to help us focus on what works and improve performance pragmatically.
