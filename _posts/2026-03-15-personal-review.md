Even if you have never written your own journal before, you have certainly seen, heard about, or met someone who does. They might do it for practical reasons, such as keeping records of past actions or remembering lessons. Others might do it as a form of self-care, such as self-talk therapy or objective reflection. Even within journaling itself, there are many different styles. Some could be categorized as the classic “Dear diary…” format. There are also gratitude journals focused primarily on altering one’s perspective. People also approach their past [notes](/2026/02/11/note-taking/) differently. Some intentionally set aside time to read through the thoughts of their past selves and recontextualize their present circumstances. Others leave them to be forgotten, or in extreme cases, erase or burn them before they die.

For those who have never started writing just for themselves, journaling can sound time-consuming and unproductive. However, when done tactically, spending only ten minutes a day reviewing your own performance can directly increase tomorrow’s productivity. With any task or skill, we need feedback to improve. If performance could be measured numerically, we would intuitively know how much more effort should be invested or which weak points need to be addressed in order to improve the result. Unfortunately, we cannot objectively score an entire day. Even if we tried, the context would collapse so much that we would no longer know which specific problems we should address or which achievements we should celebrate.

Thankfully, we do not need to create complicated dashboards to achieve adequate self-evaluation. Simply forcing yourself to turn your day into words is enough. Our minds are still reliable enough to [retain](/2026/03/24/recommending-anki/) the most significant events of the day and list them within a few minutes. By utilizing this natural ability through writing or typing, you can have a quick and productive meeting with yourself and then [return](/2026/04/10/restarting-systems/) to work immediately.

Of course, the time frame does not have to be restricted to 24 hours. You could also conduct weekly, monthly, or even quarterly reviews to give and receive feedback on long-term projects or your overall trajectory. As long as we continue monitoring ourselves objectively, we should be able to avoid major errors that require drastic course corrections and gradually remove the small inefficiencies that slow us down.

Journaling is diverse, both in content and approach. Nevertheless, the benefit of writing to yourself remains the same: it provides brief but deep feedback that can help you improve. The review timeline is also flexible enough to help manage both short-term and long-term performance. Spending a small amount of time on personal reflection can therefore become a powerful way to accelerate productivity.
