*Estimated Reading Time : 3-4min*

The Pareto principle, or for most people the 80–20 rule, states that for many outcomes roughly 80% of consequences come from 20% of causes. If you have read some productivity books, you have probably come across it more than once and seen authors praising it. The idea is definitely appealing: when applied correctly, you can work on 20% of the effort and obtain 80% of the results. Examples I have seen include solving 20% of customer complaints to clear up 80% of phone calls, or advertising to 20% of a core audience and receiving 80% of the profit from them.

For people who just want to get most of the job done, the Pareto principle can be a perfect guide. However, what if you are in a competitive environment, want to execute a task competently, or are looking for a breakthrough? How can you differentiate yourself from your peers when your work produces no more and no less than others’? Can you discover the unknown if the search only takes place in the mostly known parts? In such cases, paying attention to details might bring better outcomes than trying to be mostly efficient.

Details—especially the minor ones—are time-consuming to deal with. Furthermore, their effects are often too small to be quantified, let alone noticed. However, as you continuously make tiny [incremental](/2026/03/25/incremental-progress/) improvements, they start to add up. To most people, myself included, it is nearly impossible to identify each small piece that makes something “better” from the whole. Nevertheless, even without expertise, you and I can still intuitively feel the difference.

When it comes to new discoveries, they sometimes happen right under our noses. Whenever I hear those stories, I find myself wondering, *why can’t we systematize the research process to produce more findings faster?* In some ways it already has been institutionalized, but the cycle cannot be fully automated because it has to be inefficient for it to work. Every detail must be examined, every hypothesis needs to be crossed out, and every mental simulation must be explored before arriving at a conclusion that may have been there all along.

The Pareto principle is a powerful tool, but when used in the wrong situation it will not solve our problems. In those circumstances, contrary to most productivity advice, it might actually be better to let the details consume you. The more details you pay attention to, the higher the chance of seeing the result you are hoping for. If you manage to produce the output you desired through layers of inefficient micro-decisions, observations, and improvements, I would still consider it a productive use of time.
