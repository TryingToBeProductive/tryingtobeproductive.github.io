When you [read a book](/2026/02/27/reading/) for the first time, you take time to highlight, reread certain parts, and organize important insights into notes in order to memorize them. After going through all of this, one might believe that they have absorbed everything there is to know from the book. Yet, when reviewing the whole book, we find new connections, understand the bigger picture, and pick up [details](/2026/03/06/details/) that were missed during the first reading. Even in a casual setting, such as when we consume content, rewatching it with knowledge of the entire plot doesn't ruin the experience. In fact, it allows us to focus on and appreciate the craft, from minor creative decisions to the overarching structure.

When we try to improve our performance, we inadvertently add more components and rules to our productivity system. When this is done continuously for a few weeks, complexity increases exponentially. However, if we only check the parts that are important for our immediate tasks, our workflow seems perfect. But when we reach the downstream, we question why we only got disappointing results.

We can also see the cracks in our system indirectly by comparing our time schedule and our [time tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/) records. Of course, we have to account for the positive bias in our planning, but when we fail to live up to our own promises or feel like progress is too slow for the amount of time spent working, even under the best of conditions, there might be a structural issue.

Not only can reviewing your entire way of life help find solutions to very specific tasks, but it can also make us question inefficiencies in our systems, motivating us to look for ways to eliminate certain steps, merge work, and optimize the [quality](/2026/08/20/quantity-and-quality/)-to-quantity ratio. Especially when a workflow or parts of a process are similar enough, tips can be transferred and might allow us to multitask without increasing context switching. In addition, even when two tasks have nothing in common on the surface, if they are both facing the same problem, such as time, effort, or cost, fixing one can fix both. In other words, troubleshooting can be minimized by maximizing the scope of our analysis.

However, inspecting all the actions we take in our daily lives can be time-consuming, let alone connecting the dots, coming up with solutions, and checking the causal relationships. We would like to go through everything as seldom as possible while also wanting to patch holes as quickly as possible. In the case of large-scale introspection, a period in which your biggest project or multiple smaller projects have reached their pseudo-completion would give you a wealth of information to analyze, enough time to remake and rearrange your plans, and fewest things to keep track of when A/B testing your approaches. Of course, weekly or monthly reviews will help you course-correct during execution, but a whole-system review will build the conditions for better execution.

When we don't know what went wrong in spite of our best intentions, our focus might have been too narrow in the first place. Seemingly independent works, tasks, and projects are and can be connected or integrated both horizontally and vertically. As such, both the root cause and solutions can arise from unlikely places. Searching through everything will yield discoveries, but doing it too often prevents us from doing actual work. Fortunately, [transition](/2026/08/13/adjustment-period/) periods come periodically, giving us room to think and experiment. Productivity requires constant improvement, but it doesn't have to be small or direct. Large or indirect measures have to be considered from time to time in order to keep increasing one's efficiency and, consequently, their performance.
