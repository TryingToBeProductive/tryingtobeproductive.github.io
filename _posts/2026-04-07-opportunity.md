Fans of NASA and space exploration will always remember the legendary Mars rover, Opportunity. Its name was given by a nine-year-old girl who had endured hardship but still found hope in the sky. Her essay perfectly captured NASA’s mission: a chance to explore Mars scientifically and make breakthrough discoveries along the way. The mission exceeded all expectations, allowing humanity to operate on Martian soil for 15 years instead of the originally planned 90 days. It was an extraordinary run, and it tells a neat story about why accepting worthwhile challenges matters.

However, when we make decisions in the present, it is difficult to know whether we are truly seizing an opportunity. We may pursue better positions without having the necessary skills or resources to sustain them. Conversely, we sometimes reject promising paths because “the timing isn’t right” or because we fear failure. What we later call “a good decision” is often only recognizable in hindsight—especially in cases that involve significant life changes, such as long-term projects or career shifts. Rather than relying solely on instinct, it is more productive to plan, prepare, and act based on the limited information available. After all, accurately predicting the future remains beyond our reach.

Success can feel almost mystical. Even within the same field, many hardworking individuals never realize their ambitions. This is partly a consequence of living in a world governed by statistics. When viewed in aggregate, effort and achievement often show a correlation. Yet, from an individual perspective, outcomes can still feel random. While hard work may not be sufficient for success, it is almost always necessary. If a clear opportunity were to present itself, few would be able to take advantage of it without prior preparation.

Thinking in terms of opportunity can also become a distraction. In many everyday decisions, multiple options yield similar benefits. They may differ in form but ultimately satisfy the same set of [desires](/2026/05/01/need-vs-want/). Even after carefully evaluating each scenario and making a choice, we often remain haunted by the opportunity cost of the paths not taken. The abstract nature of these [trade-offs](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/) makes them difficult to quantify, leading to cycles of doubt and reconsideration. One effective approach is to address these internal reservations directly—answering the doubts raised by your inner skeptic one by one. This internal dialogue takes time, but achieving mental clarity is often worth the effort.

Everyone values opportunity, yet no one can clearly recognize it in the present. It does not appear as a reward waiting to be claimed. Instead, it resembles something buried—only those who prepare consistently have any chance of uncovering it. Fixating on opportunity itself, on the other hand, can create unnecessary [conflict](/2026/09/13/system-conflict/) when choosing a path forward. When uncertainty arises, it is better to confront and clarify your doubts rather than suppress them. Productivity depends on focus, and focus requires a clear mind. Resolve internal conflicts first—then turn to the work itself.
