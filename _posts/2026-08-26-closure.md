When your computer seems to be acting slower than usual, your first instinct might be to open up the terminal and check for any unnecessary programs that are eating up your RAM. Once you find the suspect, you double-check to make sure it has no essential role in your OS, and terminate it. If we just pretend for a second that our minds are like machines, a similar mental process takes place when we try to find "closure."

To have mental clarity, we outsource our thoughts to our notes and [task managers](/2026/02/22/to-do-list/). Yet, it is quite difficult not to think about work, tasks, or projects that are still in progress. Our minds try to come up with new solutions, details, and improvements to finish them, which is helpful most of the time. However, this process starts to drain our mental energy when it gets prolonged beyond its time horizon.

Some initiatives have to be retired early due to changes in circumstances, while other work has to be shipped before the [deadline](/2026/04/01/deadlines/) even if it isn't perfect. Not doing them does not mean that we won't be thinking about them. Furthermore, since those thoughts can't be acted upon, our minds can become stuck in an open loop, trying to solve an unsolvable problem.

In order to terminate our background processes, we need to give our minds a problem that they can solve: "What should the end state be?" By shifting our question from continuation to ending, we get our brains to write their own exit code and close the loop on their own. In addition, since our individual definition of "complete" does not have to be grounded in reality, we can make things up solely to put our thinking to rest. Once you experience the return of [tranquility](/2026/04/16/calm/), you can be confident that you have found your closure.

Achieving inner peace is nice, but we also need to be aware that a lack of closure can be used as a distraction. In storytelling and advertising, omitting crucial context is an old tactic used by writers to keep viewers hooked. Even when it is unintentional, news can also be considered open-ended information. No matter how much journalists, scholars, or you yourself dig into the mysteries of a given incident, there will always be undocumented material, undisclosed state secrets, and destroyed evidence that prevent anyone from attaining the "truth."

If we aren't careful, so-called "big questions" can trap us in a never-ending loop as well. Classical problems such as "What is life?", "Why are we here?", or "What is real?" have been debated for centuries. Due to the lack of progress, some philosophers have considered answering them to be beyond our cognitive capabilities. Unless your job depends on obsessing over these open-ended [questions](/2026/03/16/asking-right-questions/), ignoring them would be the better choice for your productivity.

Mental clarity is foundational to productivity. When physical tools can't remove mental parasites, artificially create logical closures so that they can self-terminate. Of course, if possible, we should also prevent outside parasites from entering our brains in the first place. Consume less media, and think small—not to have a closed mind, but to have free headspace to absorb information and think productively.
