When encountering information, knowledge, or wisdom, we either understand it or we do not. However, failing to understand something becomes especially frustrating when we sense that it contains profound insight. There are many ways to describe this feeling: something “going over our heads,” intellectual humility, or being “out of our depth.” Most people recognize that missing important [details](/2026/03/06/details/) can create problems in the future, so finding ways to overcome this gap quickly becomes valuable.

The definition of being “out of one’s depth” is straightforward: lacking the knowledge, experience, or skills necessary to deal with a particular subject or situation. Naturally, the direct solution would be to acquire those missing forms of knowledge, experience, and skill.

However, if we attempt to cover every possible gap by endlessly studying and accumulating technical expertise, we may never spend enough time actually producing meaningful results. Instead, we can narrow our focus significantly by [taking notes](/2026/02/11/note-taking/) based not on complete understanding, but on emotional reaction. Frustration itself becomes a filtering tool. If an idea feels important despite being incomprehensible, it is likely worth revisiting later. Even if our notes become filled with jargon we cannot yet understand, writing them down preserves potential future insight. What feels meaningless today may become obvious after gaining more experience.

When the subject mainly consists of objective facts, the solution is relatively clear: read relevant books and articles, ask experts, or use tools such as large language models to clarify missing information. However, subjective forms of understanding are much harder to access. In these situations, imitation may become the only practical path forward. Many instincts and intuitions are either undocumented or impossible to describe precisely enough for direct replication. By following the process step by step—even without fully understanding it—we begin to uncover the unwritten aspects of the craft. These newly gained insights help us identify what still needs to be understood, allowing the cycle of learning and refinement to continue until the knowledge either becomes integrated into our worldview or at least understandable enough to build effective countermeasures.

When we encounter ideas that go over our heads, we generally have two options. The first is the natural route: waiting until enough mistakes and experiences gradually teach us their meaning. The second is actively identifying and filling the gaps in our objective and subjective understanding after narrowing down what is missing. Although the former approach often leads to deeper and more holistic understanding, the latter is usually necessary if we want to improve performance within a shorter timeframe. Learning from experience is unavoidable, but productivity often depends on accelerating that process. Intellect is not something merely maintained; it is something continually acquired. In that sense, the feeling of defeat is not a sign to retreat, but a signal pointing toward the next stage of growth.
