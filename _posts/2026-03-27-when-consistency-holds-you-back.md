Most people prefer predictable patterns, and nothing provides more predictability than consistency. When outcomes remain stable across different situations, they give us a sense of security, reduce cognitive load, and build confidence in our responses. Depending on the task, consistent results can also help [eliminate](/2026/03/12/minimalism/) redundancies in systems that would otherwise need to handle outliers. For a biological brain that constantly prioritizes energy conservation, such ‘coherent’ structures are much easier to process. However, consistency becomes counterproductive when it is forced onto situations where it does not naturally exist.

Personalities, traits, and values are often the biggest [blind spots](/2026/05/04/blind-spots/) when building systems for getting things done. We like to believe in a “true self” that explains our motivations, behaviors, and thought processes. This belief can create internal [conflict](/2026/09/13/system-conflict/) when we interact with the real world—especially when we take on multiple roles. Even within a single day, we shift between home, work, school, and social settings, sometimes entering completely unfamiliar environments. Trying to apply the same principles in all of these contexts rarely works, because each situation presents unique stimuli and demands different responses.

This idea is not new. In sociology, Georg Simmel described how individuals in urban environments often feel “fragmented” as they adjust different aspects of themselves across social contexts. Neuroscience offers an even more unsettling perspective: some researchers argue that there is no single “master model” of the self. The brain can generate logical explanations for decisions after they are made, reconstruct memories based on current context rather than past reality, and even reshape internal models to align with future expectations. While a coherent personal narrative feels comforting, our psychology and biology are far more adaptive—and inconsistent—than we might assume.

So when you are designing a workflow, a detailed process, or a system, ask yourself: “Am I unintentionally building a solution that aligns with my personal narrative rather than the problem itself?” Some tools—both literal and conceptual—may be more effective, even if they feel inconsistent with your usual “style.” If your goal is to accomplish more tasks and improve performance, consistency should sometimes be sacrificed in favor of precision and force. Fixating on things that don’t solve your problem hinders productivity, so remove them intentionally to move forward faster.
