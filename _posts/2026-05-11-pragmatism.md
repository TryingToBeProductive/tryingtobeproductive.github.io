*Realpolitik* is often described as a pragmatic approach to international relations, most famously associated with Henry Kissinger. Of course, other notable figures either actively embraced or were forced to adopt similar approaches, including Otto von Bismarck, Deng Xiaoping, and Lee Kuan Yew. Deng Xiaoping even summarized his philosophy with the phrase: “It doesn’t matter whether a cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice.” Although realpolitik is often criticized for its lack of morality, the results it produced were undeniably significant: the unification of Germany, major achievements in U.S. foreign policy, and the transformation of a poor nation into a global superpower.

If pragmatism is such an effective tool, should we apply it to every aspect of productivity? The answer depends less on the desired outcome and more on the scale at which it is applied.

Pragmatism is powerful because thoughts and actions are constantly evaluated according to tangible results. As a consequence, fewer resources—such as time, [money](/2026/03/22/budgeting/), and energy—are wasted on ideas that may lead nowhere. Most investments generate some form of return, making pragmatism highly efficient in the short term. However, once we broaden the number of variables used to evaluate success, the picture becomes less appealing. Actions often produce unintended side effects, and when multiple goals interact with one another, these [trade-offs](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/) become difficult to ignore. Returning to the earlier historical examples, the pursuit of results also generated conflicts, human rights violations, widening inequality, and intense geopolitical competition.

There is another philosophy closely related to pragmatism: Utilitarianism. While both focus heavily on outcomes, pragmatism prioritizes proven solutions that work under real-world conditions, whereas utilitarianism evaluates actions according to their potential to maximize overall benefit. The difference is subtle but important. Utilitarianism attempts to unify multiple objectives under a broader moral vision, while pragmatism often concentrates on achieving individual goals efficiently. When pragmatism is applied too broadly to personal productivity systems, we may end up completing more tasks without experiencing meaningful progress in life overall.

In addition, excessive reliance on what has already worked can reduce experimentation and cause us to overlook better alternatives. There is almost always a more effective method somewhere, but discovering it requires time, money, and energy. Over the long term, however, even small improvements compound through repetition and eventually outperform conventional approaches.

So when should pragmatism be used? For simplicity, tasks can be divided into three categories: experimentation, operations, and systems. Pragmatism is most useful during operational work—situations that demand reliable and repeatable output. Interestingly, idealism may actually strengthen pragmatism at the other two levels. During experimentation, pursuing what could work rather than only what is already proven expands our knowledge and increases the number of methods available later. At the systems level, a unifying vision helps coordinate the various parts of life and productivity toward a common direction. This reduces redundancy, minimizes friction between [priorities](/2026/03/05/priorities/), and allows each component to function more effectively.

Pragmatism is a mindset, not a complete philosophy of life. Its effectiveness depends on applying it within the appropriate context. Moreover, even a powerful tool becomes stronger when paired with vision, experimentation, and long-term direction. The approaches we choose shape the quality of our performance, so optimizing not only our actions but also our frameworks may be one of the most important forms of productivity.
