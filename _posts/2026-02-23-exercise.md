Exercise was not always considered important for productivity. However, with the spread of fitness trends, research papers showing a positive relationship between exercise and daily performance gained more attention. You have probably heard claims such as “running while studying helps you absorb more material,” “daily exercise burns excess energy and decreases inflammation,” or “exercise triggers neuron development, increasing cognitive performance,” and so on. These ideas sound intuitive: a better brain improves mental tasks, and fewer [health](/2026/05/05/health/) issues mean fewer unwanted interruptions — both of which directly contribute to higher productivity.

However, exercise can also make your daily workload feel smaller in counterintuitive ways. As you experience after finishing a workout, physical activity makes you tired. Normally, being tired would mean it takes longer to complete the same amount of work. But this is not necessarily the case when you train regularly because of one key concept: endurance.

Modern tasks — typing, sitting, staring at a screen, thinking, or moving around for long periods — all require a certain amount of muscular support. Those muscles must sustain you throughout the day, and if they are not strong enough, the body begins to break down before your tasks are finished. In worse-case scenarios, you might even develop repetitive stress injuries (RSI), which can prevent you from working for extended periods.

By intentionally placing stress on your muscles through exercise, they become fatigued and microscopic damage occurs. When they regenerate, not only are the damaged parts repaired, but muscle strength and capacity increase enough to handle greater resistance in the future. This means that by tiring yourself through physical activity, you build the endurance needed to withstand long-term mechanical stress associated with your job. As a result, your productivity increases in both [quality](/2026/08/20/quantity-and-quality/) and quantity.

For additional context, some may wonder: why do our bodies need exercise in the first place? The reason lies in the mismatch between the speed of natural selection and technological development. Our brains are malleable enough to adapt quickly to changing environments — from hunter-gatherer societies to agriculture to modern office jobs. However, our bodies are not as adaptable. They were optimized for environments where significant physical activity was essential for survival. To increase the chances of passing on genes, our physiology evolved to perform best under regular physical strain. Yet technological advancement has removed much of that strain from our daily lives so rapidly that we now need to intentionally reintroduce it.

Exercise improves productivity in both direct and indirect ways. As long as we operate within this biological “meat suit,” we are almost required to train our bodies to realize our full potential. So I encourage you to make time for regular workouts. You will experience the benefits.
