*Estimated Reading Time : 4-5min*

Book summaries are everywhere these days. There are videos, podcasts, apps, and even paid services built around them. For fast readers, those “time-savers” may not seem very attractive. But for slow to average readers like us, they might feel life-changing — if they actually worked.

On average, it takes about 8.5 to 12 hours to casually read through a 300-page book. If you are highlighting, searching, thinking, or wrestling with difficult concepts, it might take days. You may have also heard of courses that teach you to read “faster.” They are not lying — those techniques will definitely help you skim through an entire book in minutes, but at the cost of comprehension. If you are using a book to solve your existing problems, those tricks prevent that from happening.

If speeding up is not the answer, then reducing should be, right? If you have taken any writing classes, you know that most texts consist of core ideas, explanations, and examples. With that knowledge, you might think that by collecting only the key takeaways, you can absorb a book in much less time. Again, you are correct — you could technically go through a book in 15 minutes. But what you absorb would be different from the intended material.

Summaries remove the very thing that gives meaning to a text: context. If you have been on the internet for some time, you may have come across articles or images that were “taken out of context.” What makes them difficult to detect is that they are still intelligible. As long as they are written with correct grammar and do not require insider knowledge, we can read and understand them — even if the meaning is distorted. Reading only the main points of a book produces a similar result. You will learn something, but it may not be what the book intended to teach. Do not be surprised later if the book does not help, simply because you applied incomplete or incorrect advice.

Another blind spot for people who rely on gists is that they are written by someone else — and that has implications. Even with short texts, each person will select different parts as important or insignificant. When scaled up to an entire book, there is a higher chance that your “gems” will be overlooked. Going through a whole book just to dig up a few gems sounds inefficient, but those unique insights might be exactly what you need to solve your problems and get things done.

In the long term, slow reading can actually make you faster. Especially in non-academic fields, once you have read more than three books in the same category, you have encountered most of the core ideas. As long as they stay within a single subject, many books contain similar concepts, big ideas, facts, and practical knowledge — just written differently. If you already understand those deeply, you can skim familiar sections and focus only on the unconventional parts. As a result, you can “read” faster while absorbing more than you would from summaries alone.

You can read habitually in small sessions or in larger blocks. But if you are new to a subject, try reading from front to back. Once you can fully utilize the tools and ideas from a book, you will solve problems more quickly and regain or improve productivity. Instead of depending on shortcuts, try to extract the best from books — and use that depth to become more productive.
