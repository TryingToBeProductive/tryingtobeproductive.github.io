We have to make choices every single day, and we are reminded of that fact most clearly when standing in the aisles of a supermarket. Countless products, mostly similar but different enough to make us think carefully before purchasing. Even during work, leisure, or self-improvement, we decide which task to do, which form of entertainment to enjoy, or how to develop our skills. We all know that we cannot do everything at once, so we narrow things down to a few options. However, finding the personally optimal scope can be surprisingly difficult.

Choice can sometimes masquerade as freedom. In reality, the moment options are presented to us, they essentially constrain our next course of action. Especially when none of the selections fully satisfy our objectives, making a decision hardly feels like exercising our freedom. Instead, it feels like complying with the rules of the game. Fortunately, we can regain some control by adding two more dimensions to our decision-making process: disengagement and time.

We tend to forget that we can choose not to participate. Whoever presents the options still needs their subjects to make a decision before they can act in their favor. Therefore, when we stop playing their game, we not only avoid potential regrets but also free ourselves from having to comply. Even though most of us set our own boundaries, routines, or strict workflows to ensure our daily performance, limits unwillingly imposed by others can hinder our ability to perform rather than focus it. Inaction will always feel like a form of procrastination. Nevertheless, if we want to [safeguard](/2026/03/20/dealing-with-system-breakdowns/) our productivity, walking away empty-handed is not the same as being unproductive.

Thinking in timelines also expands our choices. Most of the time, we can do tasks, acquire things, or go through processes later. This significantly clarifies our thoughts when we would otherwise be forced to choose only one option in the moment. Instead of evaluating only the best immediate outcome, we can analyze our options based on our [priorities](/2026/03/05/priorities/) and choose the second- or third-best option later. Of course, performing these calculations only in our heads can lead to procrastination. As such, writing down our intentions in advance is necessary to fully take advantage of time in our decision-making.

Last but not least, our environments can also dictate our choices. Cultural upbringing, societal status, and geographic restrictions are obvious examples. However, we still need to be aware that products, places, and content are also highly engineered to nudge us toward certain choices or limit our potential courses of action. It is difficult to make an independent judgment when a strong force is present. Most of us are familiar with the snacks placed in front of checkout lines, relevant ads in search results, and the capabilities of our work machines affecting our decisions. The line between our own thoughts and those of others can become blurry when something has been designed to appeal to us rather than being chosen because we genuinely value its features.

It is impossible to completely remove environmental factors from our decision-making process. However, we can diminish their effects by planning things beforehand. By doing so, we can isolate our thinking from the environment in which the action will take place. Fortunately, scheduling and [to-do](/2026/02/22/to-do-list/) lists already cover much of this planning process, so we should do it ahead of time to avoid being swayed by outside factors.

Making a choice is simple; making choices that actually suit our needs is complicated. There are always invisible options that we can exploit. We can also choose the process by which we make our choices in order to make better decisions. Productivity systems are built on layers of past choices while also producing future decisions. Actively question conventional rules about choice so that we can maintain better control over our present and future performance.
