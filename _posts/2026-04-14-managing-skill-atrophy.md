You may have learned complex mathematical operations or memorized world history in great detail during your school years. However, most adults—even those who remain in academia—would struggle to answer questions that their younger selves would have found easy. While it can feel discouraging to realize that you can no longer do things you once could, we are now also facing the possibility of accelerating skill atrophy through increased reliance on AI. As the saying goes, “use it or lose it.” But do we really need to invest our limited resources in maintaining skills we are not using?

Strict routines can help keep us [sharp](/2026/05/06/sharp/). Whether it is a physical ability or a intellectual skill, repeatedly using the same muscles or neural circuits reinforces them. Both muscle fibers and neural pathways strengthen over time, allowing you to replicate your past performance more instinctively.

However, the issue with adding more routines becomes apparent when you start scheduling your day through time blocks. If you subtract 8 hours of sleep, 8 hours of work, 2 hours of house chores, 1 hour of commuting, 1 hour of [exercise](/2026/02/23/exercise/), and 1 hour of eating from a 24-hour day, you are left with only 3 hours. This is realistically enough for just one or two ongoing projects. In theory, you could divide that time into six 30-minute skill-maintenance routines, but this would come at the cost of pursuing new skills or meaningful projects. [Weekends](/2026/08/17/working-on-holidays/) could also be used for this purpose, but that would come at the expense of rest and family time. In extreme cases, one might even sacrifice work productivity to make room for training.

Still, having a wide range of skills is undeniably valuable. Much like having a diverse set of tools increases the likelihood of fixing a problem efficiently, possessing multiple skills allows you to approach challenges from different angles. This flexibility can lead to faster solutions, even if the methods are unconventional.

So how can we prevent our past efforts from going to waste without neglecting the present and the future? Ultimately, it comes down to prioritization. Completing current tasks and progressing toward immediate goals should usually take precedence over preserving past abilities. Start by scheduling your time to accommodate as many goals as possible, and test whether the plan is sustainable. If it is not, temporarily drop the lowest-priority routines until you regain the capacity to [resume](/2026/04/10/restarting-systems/) them. The same applies when higher-priority projects emerge.

By setting clear priorities before planning, you can allocate your limited resources toward what matters most now and in the future—without completely abandoning skills that may not currently contribute to your work but could still prove useful.

Changes in circumstances often force us to leave behind certain abilities and adopt new ones. Nevertheless, the skills we accumulate along the way can help us overcome challenges through unconventional means. Managing skill atrophy within real-world constraints requires conscious prioritization. While we may want to pursue everything—from completing current tasks to developing new projects—time limitations make this impossible on a daily basis. Instead, use a priority-based approach over months and years, and take advantage of rare periods of excess time.

The more tools you possess, the greater your potential performance. However, preserving those tools comes at a cost. Maintain them only when you can afford that cost, ensuring both present productivity and future adaptability.
