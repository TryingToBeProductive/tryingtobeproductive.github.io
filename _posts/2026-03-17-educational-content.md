“Knowledge is power.” Most people would not immediately object to this idea. But should we spend most of our time acquiring knowledge? Even those whose jobs revolve around information management would hesitate to say yes. It is quite obvious that if we are always in the “motion stage”—periods of learning, preparing, and planning—we will never reach the “action stage,” where real work gets done. Therefore, whenever we set a timeline for a new [goal](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/), even if we are not fully conscious of it, we usually assign a time limit for gathering materials in order to ensure that things actually get completed.

However, time allocation can become tricky when it comes to collecting [ideas](/2026/05/20/ideas/), insights, and ways of thinking. Most of the time, these are not directly connected to the problem at hand, but they can unexpectedly become sources of inspiration. Especially when dealing with something outside our field, having even a small concept to hold onto can be highly beneficial. In addition, for people who work in creative domains, combining materials from unconventional sources can lead to something unique. Nevertheless, continuously searching for inspiration instead of pushing a project forward is also problematic.

Another issue that has recently emerged with the rise of MOOCs and freely available educational content is “productive procrastination.” Since we were young, we have been told that learning is good, so spending time consuming informational content feels productive. However, there are virtually infinite subjects and subfields to explore, and once we fall into that endless pool, it can consume the time needed to complete necessary tasks. What makes this more complicated is that the knowledge we gain from videos, articles, or audio content can sometimes transfer into our daily lives when the right conditions arise. As a result, such activities are not entirely unproductive.

To gain the benefits while avoiding the downsides, I have found it helpful to use a priority list along with [time tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/) and time blocking to manage unintentional exploration. In most cases, broadening one’s horizon is relatively low in priority. Therefore, when you are under time pressure, you should cut off the search for inspiration without hesitation and focus on more important tasks. When higher-priority tasks are sufficiently handled, you can then allocate time for learning without concern. Standard time-management techniques—recording and scheduling—allow you to find appropriate time slots without disrupting your work, chores, or other activities. There may be some trial and error at first, but as with most systems, it stabilizes with enough iteration.

Investing in personal education has numerous benefits, from improving skills to potentially sparking the next breakthrough idea. However, allocating too much time to learning can reduce overall output. To acquire new knowledge while maintaining performance, clear priorities and effective time management are essential. Once these [trade-offs](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/) are understood and applied consistently, learning can become a source of unexpected productivity gains when you need them most.
