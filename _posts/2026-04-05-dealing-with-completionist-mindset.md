*Estimated Reading Time : 3-4min*

Most of us want to complete our projects to feel a sense of achievement. It usually serves as a good motivator to keep us moving. However, it can sometimes be used against us and derail our progress. There are plenty of past projects or endeavors that we later realize did not align with our goals or no longer fit our current situation. Companies also take advantage of this tendency to increase sales—through collectibles, streaks, and similar mechanisms. Even when we know that completing something for its own sake is unproductive, it is quite hard to ignore the [sunk cost](/2026/08/23/making-up-for-losses/) and walk away.

Even with a detailed priority list and strict time management, there will always be a nagging feeling pushing us to complete past objectives, just to get rid of the guilt we feel toward our past selves. Completely eliminating a completionist mindset would likely do more harm than good. However, we can take steps to limit its influence in areas we no longer want to engage in.

The first step is to deprioritize the task while acknowledging that you once wanted it done. If you recall, the GTD system already has a useful section for this: the “Someday” list. By simply writing down tasks that no longer contribute to your progress, you give them a “temporary” pause. This reassures your brain that they will eventually be completed. Fighting the urge directly drains mental resources that should be used for important work, so it helps to make a vague promise to yourself: once your immediate to-do list is cleared, you will return to them. This makes it easier for your brain to cooperate.

Fortunately, over time, the desire to finish these tasks naturally fades, making them easier to ignore. However, this is also when you forget why you abandoned them in the first place, which makes you vulnerable to convincing yourself to pick them up again. There was a reason you stopped. If you can remind yourself of that reason, you can prevent your future self from repeating the same mistake. [Notes](/2026/02/11/note-taking/) can help with this—they allow you to revisit your decision-making process and rediscover why you changed direction. However, if those notes are buried under hundreds of others, they may only resurface after you relearn the same lesson the hard way. Make them searchable, or review them regularly—perhaps monthly—to keep your key insights accessible.

As time passes, your environment, tools, and needs change. What once worked or felt important can become obsolete or irrelevant. Resisting the urge to follow through on past decisions can be distracting. Instead of suppressing it, allow it to fade by satisfying your completionist mindset with temporary reassurance. At the same time, write down why you are pausing the work—not only to justify it to your current self, but also to prevent your future self from making the same mistake. Completionist tendencies need to be managed so you can [redirect](/2026/04/21/replacing/) your energy away from unproductive tasks and maintain performance during periods of [transition](/2026/08/13/adjustment-period/).
