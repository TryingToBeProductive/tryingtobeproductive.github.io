“Improvise, adapt, and overcome.” The phrase has become so ubiquitous that many people treat it more as a meme than as practical advice. Yet if the goal is to maintain or improve performance without additional investment, this principle offers a effective guide.

Its most obvious application appears when systems we rely on break down. Ideally, we would pause, diagnose the issue, and rebuild properly. But when ongoing tasks cannot be delayed, temporary solutions become necessary. In such situations, improvisation is a practical process: use whatever is available, arrange it to fit the immediate problem, and verify that the outcome remains acceptable.

In digital contexts, many problems can be resolved by combining existing tools found online and connecting them manually. In the physical world, simple materials—sometimes as basic as duct tape—can be used to construct functional, if temporary, solutions. The real challenge arises when complex hardware fails. Without the required expertise or equipment, on-site repair may not be possible. In these cases, alternatives such as renting equipment or using shared resources can allow work to continue.

Beyond [emergencies](/2026/03/20/dealing-with-system-breakdowns/), improvisation also plays a role in building resilient systems. It is often assumed that engineers aim to create complex machines. In reality, they strive to design devices that balance two extremes: minimizing system complexity while maximizing the range of problems they can solve. This becomes clear when considering failure points. The more dependencies a system has, the greater the chance that one will fail—and bring the entire system down with it. Rather than immediately adding new components or features, it is often safer to first explore whether existing elements can be adapted to handle new challenges. If a problem resembles one you have already solved, a small [adjustment](/2026/08/13/adjustment-period/) may be sufficient.

Improvisation is also useful when navigating [trade-offs](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/). Resources can be defined in many ways, but for practical purposes, they can be grouped into three categories: time, money, and experience. While there is often the "perfect" solution, it may require resources you do not currently have—such as expensive tools, extended time commitments, or specialized skills. This creates a gap between what is ideal and what is feasible. Instead of waiting for perfect conditions, it is often better to adjust your approach to fit available resources and begin immediately. The result may not be optimal in theory, but it is effective in practice.

Ultimately, effective problem-solving is less about acquiring new tools and more about making better use of what is already available. By aligning methods with existing constraints, you increase the likelihood of both starting and sustaining progress. Productivity must operate within limits, not outside them. In that sense, the principle remains relevant: improvise, adapt, and overcome—not as a slogan, but as a method for maintaining and improving performance without unnecessary cost.
