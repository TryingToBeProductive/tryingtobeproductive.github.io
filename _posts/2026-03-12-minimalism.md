“Out of sight, out of mind.” The proverb is self-explanatory: if you cannot see something, you are less likely to think about it. Sometimes this causes us to forget important tasks, but at other times, removing a physical object means one less thing competing for our attention. A mild implementation of this concept would be wearing the same clothes every day—like Steve Jobs—to reduce the number of decisions we have to make each day. The extreme version would be what people call “minimalism”: removing as much as possible from your life and keeping only the bare essentials for basic survival.

The result is easy to imagine. There will be less physical clutter and, to some extent, less mental clutter as well. However, is the freed-up cognitive space actually worth it? In some ways, it has the same effect as using a [task manager](/2026/02/22/to-do-list/) or [taking notes](/2026/02/11/note-taking/). But altering the real-world environment means sacrificing the availability of certain useful tools. Nevertheless, we are also doing something similar digitally when we choose to work [offline](/2026/03/09/recommending-offline/).

From my experience, when minimalism is used as a tool to increase daily output, we receive its benefits. But when it is practiced for its own sake, it becomes counterproductive. At the end of the day, most of us have goals that go beyond simply being anti-consumerist. As a result, everything else should be treated as a tool to advance those objectives—even if that tool is a mindset. We should also remember that one of the fastest ways to improve productivity is to invest in force-multipliers. Hesitating to acquire or maintain better force-multipliers simply because they occupy some mental space is unproductive. Especially if you start improvising to compensate for a lack of equipment, you may end up wasting even more time and cognitive energy.

Owning fewer physical possessions means worrying less about them. However, having too little can also reduce your potential output. Minimalism is simply another useful tool for achieving our [goals](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/). When the idea itself starts controlling your thought process, it may be time to step back and regain control of your [priorities](/2026/03/05/priorities/) so that you can stay on track and remain productive.
