Whether we are aware of it or not, most of our actions are done to achieve certain goals. Some of these goals are set by other people — “Quarterly Goals,” “Grades,” or “Familial Goals” — which we have to internalize and align our mindset with in order to complete what has been asked of us. However, goals made by ourselves, even though they are thought out from our own values, needs, and wants, are surprisingly often not followed through.

There are many factors that contribute to this inconsistency, such as being too flexible, unrealistic, or lacking sufficient planning. But those are not the core of the problem. The real reason is a lack of information about both the present and the future.

If you have been conscious for some time, you will quickly realize that the future is unpredictable. The longer the timeframe, the harder it becomes to imagine and plan ahead. Goals such as “New Year’s resolutions” or “Life Goals” are more likely to be abandoned than maintained when there is even a slight change in circumstances. Even within a week, situations can shift enough to make us rethink our current goals. As a result, we stop following our own promises and feel like [failures](/2026/08/18/learning-from-failures/).

On the other hand, if the timeframe is nearly zero — in other words, the present — would you be confident that you know enough to make a precise decision? Most people, including myself, would answer no. This becomes an issue when we set our goals based on our values, needs, and wants, because we do not fully understand what our values truly are, why we need something, or how our wants arose. What we set out to achieve may not align with what we should be doing deep down. Even if everything goes as we desired, the [probability](/2026/03/04/thinking-in-statistics/) of reaching our own goals is still not high enough.

So how should we set goals when we are not omnipresent? The best I can suggest is to question deeply and prepare for the unknown. Every thought originates from somewhere — either from within or from external influences. Questioning helps us discover what really lies behind our motivations and uncover hidden parts of our psyche and environment. Even when doing mental simulations, asking about details, probabilities, or alternatives forces us to confront the limitations of our imagination and avoid [blind spots](/2026/05/04/blind-spots/). This naturally transitions to preparation — specifically, fail-proofing your goals.

Once you understand what can be considered certain enough and where the limits are, you can eliminate obviously “worthless” visions and focus on building a more realistic framework. Start identifying potential pitfalls. Examine how each decision connects to your values, needs, and wants, and determine how specific it should be.

Ironically, making goals too specific can increase the likelihood of losing momentum as the timeline extends. Especially for goals that require more than three months, guidelines such as “be healthier,” “be more creative,” or “be intentional” may work better than strict mandates like “[exercise](/2026/02/23/exercise/) at least one hour per day,” “post videos every week,” or “meditate every day.” Because when circumstances — or you yourself — change, you can still align your actions with the guideline. This safeguards the goal from being abandoned.

It may sound like we are being asked to do a lot just to set a goal for self-improvement. However, when a goal truly fits both your situation and your inner core, your actions and decision-making will flow more seamlessly — and productivity will follow naturally.
