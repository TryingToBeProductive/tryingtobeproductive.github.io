*2026/04/05*

*Estimated Reading Time : 3-4min*

Most would agree that disregarding long-term consequences never ends well. However, if you plan everything on the scale of a lifetime, it becomes paralyzing. There are many temporary goals or moves that one must make to meet the demands of the outside world, regardless of personal life objectives. Even our values change, prompting decisions that would have been unthinkable in the past. Combined with the unpredictable nature of reality, it becomes almost impossible to fit every action, task, and personal system into a single narrative.

There is another downside to thinking beyond our lifetime: the erosion of [meaning](/2026/09/18/meaning-decay/). We like to imagine that our work will carry on from one generation to the next, creating a kind of domino effect that allows us to leave a mark on the fabric of the universe. However, there is an opposing force working against this idea: entropy. Not only does it dismantle order, but it also erases information by gradually mixing all matter into a uniform state. This can be motivating for some—after all, even your mistakes won’t be remembered. On the other hand, those who think in terms of individual impact may find this perspective discouraging, since any mark we leave may ultimately fade away.

Nevertheless, we cannot avoid confronting the bigger picture when planning for the week or making a career change. The challenge is to incorporate long-term thinking without becoming overwhelmed or nihilistic. One useful concept here is resolution. Naturally, we can imagine what to do today much more clearly than what to do ten years from now. We can use this difference to our advantage by keeping a low-resolution image of the future while maintaining flexibility in the present. When the image is vague, it leaves more room for interpretation. For tasks that simply need to be done, this lack of [clarity](/2026/05/27/clarity/) prevents us from overanalyzing their connection to our long-term vision, allowing us to focus on execution instead of becoming existential.

There is one problem with this approach: it makes us more susceptible to present bias. Tasks that can be completed immediately often feel more rewarding than projects that take months or years to show results. Setting rigid long-term goals can sometimes backfire, causing us to procrastinate or quit when circumstances change. What we need instead are systems—routines, guidelines, or even feedback loops—that carry us forward while keeping us grounded in the present.

We cannot always live entirely in the moment, but we also cannot let our minds be consumed by the future. We don’t need to know every detail of what will happen next. An abstract image of the future is often enough to guide present actions while allowing for [adjustment](/2026/08/13/adjustment-period/) along the way. It also allows tasks to be completed independently of a fixed “destiny.” At the same time, we must avoid discounting the future and overvaluing the present. Build systems that act like an autopilot, guiding you toward distant goals that your brain struggles to handle on its own. Being trapped inside your own thinking can be unproductive—so instead, adjust the scale of your thinking and rely on systems to reduce unnecessary mental load and maintain performance.
