“We need more data.” “We require more details.” “We need to organize this better.” No matter what kind of work we do, these phrases never fail to appear, whether during intense discussions or casual conversations. Even in our personal projects, we may not say them out loud, but we still take actions aimed at uncovering the underlying causes of our failures, doubts, and [ambiguity](/2026/04/08/facing-arbitrarlity/) in an attempt to clear our mental fog.

It could be argued that most productivity tools and trends revolve around achieving mental clarity. Writing, [note-taking](/2026/02/11/note-taking/), scheduling, [tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/), daily reflection, personal rules, routines, and meditation all serve this purpose. When combined into a unified system, they form rigid structures designed to identify and eliminate uncertainty from every corner of our lives. However, this pursuit comes with a trade-off: time.

From a long-term perspective, investing time into research, reflection, and inspection can be justified through the compounding benefits of improved performance and efficiency. Still, when we encounter a bottleneck that could theoretically be solved through additional research or prolonged thinking, solving it can become the bottleneck itself. Some data are collected improperly. Some questions remain unanswerable even for experts. Some processes cannot be done in a straightforward manner. These difficulties can certainly become learning opportunities, but compared to our actual goals, their value is sometimes questionable.

Clarity provides long-term benefits, but it can also disrupt our present schedule. Fortunately, we have a tool for this problem as well: time limits. Naturally, their strictness depends on how time-sensitive our situation is. Nevertheless, when we fail to deepen our understanding of a problem or discover satisfying solutions within the allotted time frame, the limit acts as a wake-up call to change our approach. We can [compromise](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/), move forward despite incomplete information, or promise ourselves that we will return to the issue later. When we fall too deeply into a rabbit hole, we often forget why we entered it in the first place. Just as we set alarms to wake ourselves for work, we should also set limits to pull ourselves out of endless optimization.

If we allowed ourselves to, we could dedicate all of our resources to optimization and none to actual output. To prevent this, resources — especially time — must be budgeted carefully. Productivity cannot be measured solely through long-term potential. We still need to produce something in the present to truly call ourselves "productive". Time reminds us of our [priorities](/2026/03/05/priorities/), so we should use it to keep ourselves in check.
