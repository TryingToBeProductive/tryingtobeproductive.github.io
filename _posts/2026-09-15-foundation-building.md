*Estimated Reading Time : 4-5min*

People tend to use a house as an analogy to explain the importance of a foundation. It is quite easy to imagine that, without solid ground, a building might sink, crack, or fall down altogether. Yet, barely anyone talks about HOW to build the base. The general attitude tends to be, "You will figure it out along the way." I mean, we do stumble upon some tips and tricks while working through a project. However, we can hardly call this efficient, let alone productive. Furthermore, foundation building is usually the dullest part of any work since you are at the furthest point from your goal while still figuring out how to even reach it.

Unpleasantness can demotivate anyone, so let's reduce it by solving the "figuring out" part of the process. To use the house analogy from earlier, architects need to define the material of the substructure before designing the ground itself. Identifying what will become the cornerstone of your operations is difficult, even more so when you are a beginner since you have no hindsight. As such, a logical first step would be research or mimicry.

You [read books](/2026/02/27/reading/), documents, protocols, and ask people around you to find potential building blocks. If you could discover them or manage to make a catalog of them, you would have the instructions to build your base. If not, the second-best option is mimicry. You copy the actions of someone who is comfortable with their work, write down their workflow in [detail](/2026/03/06/details/), and supplement it with your own insights. With enough notes, you might see a pattern: overlapping ideas, elements, warnings, frequency, and level of difficulty. As a result, you can use your own writing as research material to find potential building blocks and make your own instructions for forming the base.

Before setting up the timeline, we need to define the meaning of "solidified." When do you reach the point where you have memorized every step, or when mistakes are barely made? Is it when it becomes muscle memory, or is it enough to be intellectually familiar with the process? Pinpointing the sufficient target to start your project can turn your training time from a week to a few months and vice versa. In addition, the daily practice section can change from a single long hour to short but repetitive sessions.

The final piece needed to make the foundation construction work is to analyze your feedback mechanism. Without saying, the quicker the feedback, the faster you improve. However, if the task requires a few days to a few months to see the result, it is highly likely that progress will be slow or nonexistent. For example, consider the time you choose your candidate and the time you actually see their performance firsthand, or drugs that take a month to possibly show their effects. If you realize early on that the feedback mechanism is broken, you shift your metrics to quick and clear ones in order to give yourself the chance to adjust.

To maximize productivity even during your basic training, plan ahead. Your own mini-curriculum, [goal-setting](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/), time schedules, and feedback mechanisms can be designed, evaluated, and adjusted by starting with research and data collection. Of course, it won't be perfect from the start, but you would have the metaphorical groundwork to better build the actual groundwork. A foundation is required for any project, but it is not the project itself. Go through it efficiently so that you can start your real work even a day earlier.
