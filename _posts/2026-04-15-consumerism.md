Most of us hate [advertisements](/2026/03/22/recommending-ad-blockers/), yet we willingly participate in what they promote: consumerism. If we pause for a moment, we often realize that we consume far more than necessary. Do we really need to buy that candy, own high-performance hardware, or even watch endless online entertainment? Frankly, no. But we want these things, and society actively encourages this desire, perpetuating a never-ending cycle of consumption. As if that were not enough, producers have framed purchasing as a way to signal identity, adding an unnecessary social dimension to the act.

The productivity sphere is also guilty of this. It sells both [analog](/2026/03/26/analog-distraction/) and digital tools that make you look busy or sophisticated, rather than reducing workload or simplifying processes.

Attention, time, and money are all precious resources that should be used to improve performance—not extracted by profit-driven corporations. The first step is to interrupt the psychological manipulation behind consumer behavior. Installing ad blockers is a quick start, but it is not sufficient. We must also minimize exposure to new products, both online and offline.

Online platforms such as Amazon, eBay, Etsy, the Play Store, and the App Store constantly push new products. In the physical world, department stores, supermarkets, and convenience stores do the same. Make access to these environments more difficult by limiting casual browsing, both online and offline. If possible, set spending limits through your payment provider to create friction when making purchases. If you are unaware of an option, you are unlikely to consider it.

In addition, we must be cautious of online auctions. Auctions are among the most powerful psychological tools sellers use, and they are increasingly embedded into platforms like YouTube and TikTok—spaces typically associated with low-cost entertainment. When combined with easy access and competitive instincts, this creates a perfect environment for impulsive consumption. Therefore, further limiting exposure to such platforms is essential.

Once external influences are reduced, we must address internal desires. Distinguishing between needs and wants is difficult because it involves subjective judgment rather than objective analysis. Even if we list pros and cons, our brains can construct pseudo-logical arguments to rationalize unnecessary purchases.

A more effective approach is to delay decisions. By attempting to live without the desired item for a period of time, you effectively run an experiment to test its necessity. You may discover workarounds or eliminate the [dependency](/2026/05/08/dependency/) altogether. If, after several days, the problem persists despite your efforts, you can conclude that you need help.

Consumerism has built the modern economy while simultaneously draining our valuable resources. To reclaim what is rightfully ours, we must control both what enters our minds and what leaves our wallets. Applying digital and physical filters reduces exposure to irrelevant products, while slowing down purchasing decisions creates space for deliberate thinking. Productivity depends on time, money, and focus—so preserve them for your own growth, not for someone else’s profit.
