*2026/03/20*

*Estimated Reading Time : 3-4min*

No matter how resilient our systems are, when enough stress is applied or essential parts are missing, they break. Sometimes the collateral damage is local—minor hiccups in the workflow. Other times, the effects are so significant that they impair our usual routines or disrupt our tight schedules. Breakdowns are inevitable, so we should prepare contingency plans.

As in most situations, when there is a fire, the first [priority](/2026/03/05/priorities/) is to contain its spread. There will always be invisible cracks that only become apparent when it is too late to fix them. They may reveal themselves gradually or suddenly through an unexpected trigger. As soon as we notice them, we must prioritize saving what remains rather than reacting hastily. If a problem obstructs the flow of a task, the task should be temporarily abandoned to ensure that other work gets done. If completing the rest of the day’s routine becomes unlikely, at the very least, protect your [sleep](/2026/03/08/bedtime-procrastination/) to guarantee an effective reset for the next day.

Once the fire has been put out, we can begin to examine the cause. It may stem from overreliance on tools that were not up to the task, or from systems that depended too heavily on willpower and shut down as soon as that energy ran out. It could also be that a system worked well in a previous environment but became unsuitable after a shift in circumstances. In the worst case, the routines may not have aligned with our goals in the first place. By analyzing the issue from multiple perspectives, we can arrive at several plausible explanations that lead to effective solutions. The last thing we want is for the same problem to occur again in the middle of our day. Therefore, we should not hesitate to invest a few hours in identifying fixes, replacements, or eliminations to prevent future issues.

However, to manage and extinguish these “fires,” we must have sufficient buffer in our schedule. Ideally, this is similar to having emergency savings. Crises can only be resolved by spending resources—in this case, time and mental energy. These reserves can be built by setting aside a block of time each day or a dedicated day each week. Simply being prepared can significantly reduce the damage caused by system breakdowns.

When things go wrong, safeguarding what remains should take priority over immediate reaction. At the same time, problems can become opportunities to reevaluate the strength of our systems—or even reconsider their necessity. By committing to finding solutions that truly work, we can prevent future disruptions. However, course correction requires fuel. Counterintuitively, scheduling time for emergencies can preserve performance and even enhance productivity.
