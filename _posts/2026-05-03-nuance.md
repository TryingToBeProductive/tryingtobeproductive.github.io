If you are interested in business or marketing, you may have heard of the “rainy-day taxi problem.” Taxi drivers often set a daily income target to avoid working indefinitely. However, on rainy days, demand increases, which reduces the time needed to reach that target. As a result, drivers stop working earlier—even when demand is at its peak. Consequently, when customer demand is highest, the supply of taxis is lowest, leading to missed revenue for drivers and frustration for passengers.

After hearing this story, some might quickly conclude that the lesson is to avoid setting daily income goals altogether. However, this interpretation ignores why drivers adopt such limits in the first place. They work to support themselves and their families, not to maximize income. If they continue working beyond a certain point, their actions begin to misalign with their original purpose. In this sense, a daily income target is not a flaw but a practical constraint that protects their time and well-being.

So what is the real lesson? Simply put: nuance. Under normal conditions, the income target works well. But in unusual situations—such as bad weather—the goal remains the same while the calculation changes. On these days, drivers can earn more within the same number of hours, allowing them to build a buffer for future needs. Even if they reach their daily income target early, working a bit longer can better serve their original goal—supporting themselves and their families over time—by building emergency funds for their troubling times. However, this does not mean they should overwork on ordinary days, as their objective is not to maximize income at the expense of everything else.

Rules like daily income targets emerge from [trade-offs](/2026/04/02/trade-offs/)—time spent earning money versus time spent at home. These trade-offs are valid, but when conditions change, the balance between them shifts. Without adjusting the rule, what was once optimal becomes inefficient. Unlike rigid rules that produce one-size-fits-all decisions, nuance allows us to define conditional responses—clear “if–then” structures that adapt to different situations. Once we understand how our goals interact with varying conditions, we can act according to a pre-designed framework rather than reacting impulsively in the moment.

That said, developing nuance requires time and cognitive effort. As the earlier discussion suggests, examining [default](/2026/03/18/questioning-default/) behaviors, identifying changing conditions, and justifying alternative actions can quickly expand into a complex line of reasoning. It is unrealistic to expect ourselves to process and [retain](/2026/03/24/recommending-anki/) all of this mentally. Writing these thoughts down—on paper or digitally—not only clarifies the logic but also reduces the mental burden of decision-making. We tend to default to simplistic ideas because they require less effort; lowering the barrier to deeper thinking helps counteract this tendency.

Thinking through nuance in advance allows us to act more accurately in real time. Although we recognize its benefits, we often avoid complexity in favor of simplicity. Writing enables us to offload that complexity and revisit it when needed. Our productivity systems must operate within changing environments. Rather than forcing the environment to fit our workflow, we should design process that adapt to the situation so we can take advantage of changing conditions. Small improvements in decision-making compound over time, leading to meaningful gains in long-term performance.
