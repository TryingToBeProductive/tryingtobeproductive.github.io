There are plenty of statistical errors we need to be aware of, and Survivorship Bias is one of the more notable ones. You have probably heard the story in many contexts: if a plane survives an enemy attack, the parts that were damaged are actually non-lethal, while the parts that were not damaged should be reinforced instead. Similarly, for every successful singer, there are hundreds or thousands of people who never make it. If you do a little research, you will find many examples across diverse situations in which being aware of this logical fallacy can save a significant amount of time and effort.

Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) is one of the universal emotions that almost everyone experiences at least once in their lifetime. It became especially prevalent with the arrival of Social Media. Now we can see people we have never even met seemingly having a great time while we are simply trying to grind through boring but important tasks. Most advice for countering FOMO can be summarized as “lower your expectations.” In fact, if you have ever participated in some of those “exciting activities,” you might be surprised by how ordinary the reality actually feels. However, it can be difficult to think rationally when peer pressure kicks in. In such cases, understanding survivorship bias can help you regain perspective.

Consider one simple question: do you usually tell others about your boring work, routine activities, or chores? Most people would probably answer no. Yet many people happily share their best moments, exciting experiences, or ambitious plans. If everyone talks about how much fun they are having, while almost no one talks about how uneventful their past few days have been, public conversations quickly become filled with extraordinary highlights. When someone says, “Everyone is doing it,” what they often mean is simply, “Everyone is talking about it.” You can also check how common something really is by looking up relevant statistics and comparing them to the total population. In many cases, it becomes clear that most people are not doing it—or in some situations, are not even aware of it.

Our mental energy is limited, and wasting it on FOMO is the last thing we want. Even when peer pressure appears strong, understanding that what we see and hear is already heavily distorted and by paying attention to the parts of reality that go unmentioned, it becomes much easier to dismiss the irrational fear. Recognizing survivorship bias allows us to see the situation more clearly and stay focused on what actually counts.
