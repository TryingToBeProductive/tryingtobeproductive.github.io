Before Christmas, we are often asked what we would like as a gift. Strangely, many of us suddenly feel as though we do not want anything. Of course, this is not entirely true—we often want many things, sometimes even everything, as our browsing history would suggest. However, when asked to choose just one, we begin to reflect on our desires and reassess them through the lens of necessity, as if the choice must be justified.

The paradox lies in the relationship between needs and wants. Our basic needs are tied to survival: Food, water, air, shelter, and safety. We cannot live without them, so we devote time and energy to securing them. In contrast, most of what we “want” consists of things that are desirable but not essential, ranging from the latest gadgets to travel experiences. Because these wants are not tied to survival, they can feel difficult to justify as necessary. As a result, when forced to choose, we struggle—not because we lack desires, but because there is no clear overlap between what we need and what we want. Instead of asking what we want, they should have asked what we need—because only then can we distinguish between what is essential, and what is desirable.

If we move beyond purely physical necessities, the relationship becomes more complex. Using Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs as a starting point, we see that human needs extend beyond survival to include belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. Rather than existing as a simple hierarchy, these needs often operate simultaneously, competing for our attention.

These psychological needs give rise to specific desires: joining a community, improving skills, or building a meaningful career. To pursue these goals, we identify necessary actions—participating in group activities, acquiring resources for training, or seeking high-impact opportunities. From there, new wants emerge: leading the group, finding better mentors, or solving larger problems. Each of these, in turn, generates additional requirements—confidence, financial resources, knowledge, and creativity.

As a result, needs and wants cannot be cleanly separated. They form interconnected loops that expand across multiple layers. This explains why our task lists can grow rapidly and become overwhelming. However, this structure also provides a solution. If we identify and eliminate higher-level desires—the ones upstream in the chain—we can remove entire branches of downstream tasks. When you feel overwhelmed, trace your tasks back to their origin. If a root desire is unnecessary or misaligned, removing it can create significant relief.

So far, we have focused on needs and wants that stem from physical and psychological requirements. However, another category that does not fit neatly into either needs or conventional wants: motivations driven by pure desire. These do not arise from necessity, but from the way we relate to time itself. On one end, there is passion—the desire to do something simply because it is enjoyable in the present moment, even without justification. On the other, there is the drive to leave a legacy—the attempt to extend our existence beyond death through lasting impact. One is rooted in immediate experience, the other in an imagined future.

Like other desires, these motivations can also generate complex chains of actions. Yet because both operate outside the logic of necessity, evaluating them through the lens of “need” will always make them appear irrational. Instead, they should be viewed as sources of energy. Their motivational power is difficult to replace, and when harnessed effectively, they can significantly increase overall output.

Understanding the distinction—and the interaction—between needs and wants is essential for managing both present actions and future plans. By tracing the chains of our motivations and identifying their origins, we can better control the expansion of our commitments. Through clarification and exploitation of what drives us forward, we can put it to productive use and improve our performance while keeping our well-beings high.
