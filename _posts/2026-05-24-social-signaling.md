*Estimated Reading Time : 5min*

Being social creatures helped humanity build the civilization we live in today. However, the same tendencies that allow cooperation and collective progress can also be exploited for the benefit of others or lead us toward actions that are mostly performative.

Marketing departments understand very well that only a small portion of customers truly care about the technical [details](/2026/03/06/details/) of a product. Most consumers purchase non-essential goods not because of the craftsmanship behind them, but because those products communicate identity, taste, or status. Ironically, the people who understand a product most deeply are not always the ones best at selling it. They may be interested in engineering, while most cutomers are interested in social perception.

Even when money is not involved, people still engage in behaviors that produce little tangible value, especially on the internet. Corporations are frequently criticized for performative “greenwashing” campaigns, but at least the public relations teams behind them are being paid to do their job. Ordinary users, meanwhile, often repeat already popular opinions not necessarily to educate others or spread awareness, but to signal virtue or social alignment. From an evolutionary perspective, publicly demonstrating moral reliability may once have improved survival by reducing the risk of exclusion from the group. However, performing the same behavior on largely anonymous platforms raises questions about its utility.

Similar patterns appear elsewhere as well. Some people engage in displays of exaggerated toughness or “realism” not to solve problems pragmatically, but to project a strong image. Others perform self-criticism less to improve themselves and more to neutralize potential [criticism](/2026/05/10/devils-advocate/) before it appears. In both cases, social signaling can begin replacing genuine reflection or effective action.

It is difficult to deny that social behaviors which improve neither cooperation nor performance can become counterproductive. Still, completely ignoring how our actions are perceived by others is nearly impossible due to our biological wiring. Ironically, becoming tactically selfish may help us think more rationally by reducing the influence of unnecessary social validation.

The phrase “think for yourself” is often associated with competitive environments, but it also applies to the quieter moments when we think about opinions in ways that benefit neither ourselves nor others. Paradoxically, becoming less preoccupied with external perception can reduce the tendency to place ourselves at the "center of the world". As a result, decisions become less about “How do I appear?” and more about “Does this actually work?” This shift often leads to clearer thinking and less wasted effort.

No matter how impressive a cognitive tool may be, it should not dominate situations where it serves no useful purpose. Theory of mind—the ability to imagine the thoughts of others—is one of humanity’s greatest strengths. Yet without careful management, it can gradually infiltrate every part of our decision-making process, causing us to optimize for appearance rather than performance. Productivity often requires cooperation, but not social attention. We should think socially when collaboration is necessary, but think independently when making individual decisions. Sometimes the clearest path emerges only after we stop imagining a nonexistent audience.
