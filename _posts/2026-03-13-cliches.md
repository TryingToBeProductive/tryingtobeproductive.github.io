If you have consumed a fair amount of fiction, you may have come across stories, archetypes, or narrative tricks that have been overused. You may also have seen authors deliberately use the word “cliché” to signal to their readers that they are aware of what they are doing. Even in the real world, we encounter many sayings, ideas, or incidents that feel all too familiar. The experience can sometimes become so similar that some people even wonder whether they are experiencing déjà vu instead.

So how do these “tropes” help us become more productive? The answer lies in their predictability. People groan when they see something cliché because they already know how it is going to end. However, one of the major disruptors of our plans, routines, or lives in general is how unpredictable the future is. As a result, simply knowing the curve ahead of time can significantly reduce the mental energy spent on fail-proofing. In addition, either due to biological progression or societal expectations, we are often forced to face “cliché” problems, roles, or even thought patterns that have been repeated across generations. Fortunately, being aware of them and paying a bit of attention beforehand can prevent us from having to deal with them on the fly.

Of course, not everyone will experience the same narrative, hear the same phrases, or form the same logical arguments. Each person is far too multidimensional for that. Even well-known phenomena such as a “midlife crisis” or sayings like “money can’t buy happiness” become questionable under close scrutiny or technical interpretation. However, thanks to the large accumulation of human wisdom, we have many conceptual building blocks that can fit into our present or future circumstances. Even though there will always be some guesswork in selecting them—and some effort required to broaden our perspective—when used effectively, they can become powerful shortcuts for planning, decision-making, or analysis.

“Clichés” are frowned upon in artistic works for being too obvious. But this kind of obviousness can actually be advantageous when dealing with the present and beyond. Everyone lives differently, yet there are countless recorded life experiences that we can exploit. So perhaps we should unironically embrace clichés as a way to bypass unnecessary trial and error. After all, any insight that helps us make better decisions is a tool worth using in the pursuit of productivity.
