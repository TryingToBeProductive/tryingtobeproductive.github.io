Even when we try to create rules that serve a single purpose—self-betterment—we sometimes get caught off guard by underlying conflicts hidden between them.

For example, let's say that, in order to increase our savings, we enforce a spending cap on food. At the same time, to improve our [health](/2026/05/05/health/), we intentionally try to increase the variety of our food sources. Both are important for daily performance: financial margin can reduce anxiety, while a functional body is necessary for good work. However, the cost of eating healthy can hurt the savings goal, while the spending cap limits the ingredients available to us.

Sometimes, the conflict can stem from the definition itself. If we assume that deep work means doing hard or challenging work completely uninterrupted, then "hard or challenging work," which is usually full of friction, becomes the problem. We have created a rule that conflicts with another part of the same rule.

We tend to set up rules for multiple domains in our lives to better control each of them. However, the greater the distance between these domains, the less likely we are to realize the underlying conflict within our productivity system. Although it rarely happens, when we are confronted with such a conflict during execution, it can become an excuse to break both rules temporarily until we find a solution.

To prevent [self-sabotage](/2026/05/25/capable/) through internal errors, we need to develop a decision tree for quick resolution. When we try to settle our inner contradictions, we tend to do so in three ways: compromise or moderation, temporarily giving up one of the rules, or adding more rules.

Compromise or moderation is usually not the best solution, but it is the most efficient form of troubleshooting. Using the earlier examples, we can slightly increase the spending cap and limit the variety of ingredients so that we are at least saving some money while still trying to maintain a healthy diet and improve our daily performance. Or we can tweak the definition of deep work to focus on tasks that are at an optimal level of difficulty, so that we don't feel like we have failed whenever we cannot focus on challenging problems.

Conflicts always require at least two entities, which could be statements, parties, or software packages. Therefore, eliminating one eliminates the problem altogether. Returning to the earlier examples, we could put a hold on spending lavishly on food until we reach financial stability. And we could stop thinking about achieving deep work until we become familiar with the process. The rules are still being followed, but there is a time lag between them. If your only concern is the end state, then this approach is both quick and reassuring.

The final method is the most time-consuming yet most adaptive. Similar to how adding a few more definitions and parameters can help fix an error in a script, better defining the scope and options surrounding a given rule can promote coexistence between seemingly contradictory principles. Applying this to the earlier examples, if we can find a food market or a time slot that allows us to acquire a variety of foods at a low price, both rules can be followed without conflict. If we can highly optimize the working environment, necessary resources, and tools, the remaining friction might become so low that even a brand-new challenge could be tackled with deep focus. The solution would take time to materialize, but we would be compromising nothing.

Personal progress comes in many forms, and the mechanisms behind them can unexpectedly crash into each other. Rather than giving up entirely, use your own crash [protocol](/2026/04/16/templates/) to maintain and repair your system. The repair might be short, long, or even complex. Nevertheless, the further you look into productivity, the more you realize that it requires many elements to work together. Don't get discouraged when your system breaks. Working through the problems is how you achieve actual improvement.
