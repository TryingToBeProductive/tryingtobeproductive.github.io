“Sleeping on brushwood and tasting gall” is a Chinese idiom describing someone who endures hardship and imposes discipline on themselves in order to achieve a long-term [goal](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/) or seek revenge — essentially, enduring hardship and remembering past humiliation to fulfill an ambition.

The story is as follows. During China's Spring and Autumn period, Gou Jian, the King of Yue, was defeated by King Fuchai of Wu. Rather than dying in battle, Gou Jian surrendered. He and his wife were taken to Wu, where he was stripped of his title and forced to work as a lowly servant as a form of humiliation. However, due to Fuchai's eventual leniency, Gou Jian was released and allowed to return to Yue. Filled with vengeance, he deliberately kept his humiliation fresh in his mind. He slept on a bed of rough brushwood instead of a proper bed and hung a gallbladder above his seat, tasting its bitterness before meals. These rituals eventually became the source of the idiom.

Over the years, Gou Jian quietly rebuilt Yue's strength by strengthening its economy and military. When the time came, Yue's forces struck back and decisively defeated Wu. Fuchai, defeated and humiliated in turn, eventually took his own life.

As a teaching device, emphasizing the rituals that kept him reminded of his failures makes the story stick. Yet, even from this summary, one cannot help but think that his success was more likely due to his smart policies than his desire for revenge. Furthermore, due to the adaptive nature of our minds and bodies, any reminders — from sticky notes on your fridge and inspirational posters to regular [exercises](/2026/02/23/exercise/) — eventually fade into the background, as the pain they trigger gradually diminishes. Therefore, it is difficult to determine how much his humiliation rituals actually helped keep his memories [sharp](/2026/05/06/sharp/).

Productivity systems are built to increase our daily performance by making workflows efficient, balanced, and resilient. In addition, once a system is fully formed, you no longer need emotional triggers to produce results; you only need to follow the instructions you have made for yourself.

With some deliberate thought, failures can also be incorporated into productivity systems, much like how Gou Jian strengthened his kingdom through institutional changes. You can start by installing fail-safe mechanisms to prevent the same mistakes from happening again. After that, you can examine the process to remove unnecessary parts and add reinforcements to improve resource usage. Finally, look at the relationships between the different components and make the system self-sustaining. Even after going through all of that, if you still need a motivational reminder, add it to your [routines](/2026/02/20/routines/). Even when its effect disappears with time, the system will remain by your side.

Bad memories sting, which makes them powerful reminders. However, most people have experienced that wanting to change is different from actually making a change. Systems, especially those that have been emotionally detached, keep pushing you forward without requiring you to relive your wrongdoings. Enduring hardship and imposing discipline on yourself are required for success of any kind, but emotional distress alone is not sufficient. The real work lies in continuous execution and planning. If you want to truly learn from your failures, systematize them.
