To make up for the regrets caused by taking no action, the phrase “the second-best time is now” is often thrown around in productivity circles. At first glance, the logic is sound: you cannot turn back time, and problems will not solve themselves, so you need to make amends with your past and future selves. However, as with most things, context matters, and this phrase is no exception.

When you make an error in a procedure, whether it is a delivery method, an assembly process, or a bug in the code, covering it up or delegating it to your future self usually does not end well. Most of us have already experienced this exact scenario or something similar firsthand. Therefore, using the phrase as a reminder to fix mistakes quickly, even when the process is cumbersome, is appropriate if we want to maintain performance down the line.

On the other hand, when talking about a new initiative, typical examples would include investing, taking on your dream projects, or trying to acquire long-sought-after items. In these cases, the phrase can be more deceptive than helpful. Especially when it is used by marketers, it becomes difficult to distinguish between honest advice and another “call to action” tactic. They want to profit from your decision, so they are incentivized to “help” you take immediate action.

Without saying it out loud, most people understand that making decisions based on regret produces poor results. Sometimes, inaction can even save you from further trouble in the long run. To use investing as an example, investing early would, in theory, give you more opportunities to buy low and sell high. However, the same [FOMO](/2026/03/15/survivorship-bias-and-fomo/) is also the reason why many people buy high and sell low. During periods of economic growth, everyone tries to cash in on the ride, which drives up prices. When a recession hits, many people struggle to cover their expenses, forcing them to sell their assets at a loss just to survive. You could also argue that simply building an emergency fund during the same period would have been a better choice, even when inflation is taken into consideration.

Every [nuance](/2026/05/03/nuance/) and caveat must be examined before making a decision, which makes this approach incompatible with immediate action. Research and study take time, and initially, it may feel as though we are not fixing our problems or are postponing our goals and dreams. Fortunately, after [reading](/2026/02/27/reading/) a book or two, such thoughts tend to disappear because we begin to understand the risks hidden within each action.

Fixing a mistake in an existing procedure is different from taking a new action, even if both are motivated by the feeling of “I wish I had done it sooner.” The former usually rewards you with less headache in the future, while the latter could be a trap waiting for its next prey. Regret is a powerful motivator, but it cannot be the sole driving force behind your decisions. It still has to contend with logic if we want to avoid creating more regrets in the future. Productivity consists not only of what you do, but also of what you choose not to do. Action and inaction are both tools to be used wisely. Manage your feelings through them to protect your performance.
