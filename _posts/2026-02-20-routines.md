If you have ever researched how to improve your life in general, you may have heard multiple times how important “routines” are and how they can change your life. As a result, you may have tried a couple of “good” routines to better yourself, but they did not last long enough for you to experience the benefits. The truth is, when it comes to increasing productivity, routines are just another tool and cannot be done for their own sake without feeling like you are wasting your time. In addition, if you use them incorrectly, you may end up in a worse position than before. As with any power, they should be wielded responsibly.

The reason routines are recommended so often is similar to why most people recommend saving [money](/2026/03/22/budgeting/) regularly. By dividing up a task and putting in consistent effort, you can achieve greater results without bearing too much workload at once. Tasks that require long-term commitment, such as acquiring knowledge or increasing fitness, cannot be accomplished in a week. Even if you managed to do so, the physical and mental cost would be so high that it would cancel out any benefit gained from the activity.

As with saving money, spreading out effort is better than concentrating it all at once because you can let the “dividends” help you along the way. It could also be said that repetition leads to mastery. By following the same workflow repeatedly as a routine, you gain experience, discover better methods, and build muscle memory. The same actions begin to feel easier over time, and when you start feeling bored, you can level up, allowing you to extract more value from the same amount of time.

You may already understand why routines are effective, yet still wonder why it is so hard to stay on track. The first reason is that your actual goals and your routines do not align. A habit does not automatically become good or bad just because others say so. A habit can only be considered good if it truly serves your needs — which, most of the time, means serving your [goals](/2026/02/13/goal-setting/). Even though a routine may not take much time each day, over time you are investing a significant portion of your life forming and maintaining it. Therefore, you should spend time weighing the pros and cons before committing.

The second reason is what many people call the “middle slump.” When you are trying to accomplish a large goal, the beginning and the end are often filled with motivation because you can envision yourself reaching new heights after overcoming the obstacle. However, the middle phase is when motivation drops, and external pressure becomes the only force pushing you forward. This sudden decrease in internal drive is usually caused by a lack of visible progress and the feeling that the process will never end. Routines are even more challenging because there is no clear endpoint — only continuous improvement.

It may sound contradictory, but the best advice I can give is to create small end goals. Once a habit is fully formed in the brain as a neural circuit, it requires significant effort to dismantle it. If you place enough small milestones at appropriate intervals, you can maintain your new routine long enough for it to become harder to break than to keep.

I hope I have convinced those who remain unconvinced that routines are powerful tools for increasing productivity and helped you avoid the major pitfalls — so that you can experience firsthand the benefits of well-built routines.
