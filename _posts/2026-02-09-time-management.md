If you have consumed any books or podcasts about productivity, you have probably heard how important time management is. You may also have come across terms like *“time tracking,”* *“hyper-scheduling,”* and so on. When you are already busy with daily tasks, being asked to plan and track all of your time sounds overwhelming. However, as someone who has tried it for a month, I can assure you that the productivity gains from spending extra time thinking about “time” itself are worth it.

Starting with tracking all of your time, you may be skeptical about its effects. If you were like me, you might think it would decrease efficiency by making you intentionally work longer just to feel “productive.” However, the great thing about tracking not just part, but *all* of your time, is that you can clearly see how increased work hours affect other aspects of life, such as [sleep](/2026/03/08/bedtime-procrastination/), household chores, and relationships. As a result, you are forced to confront your inefficiencies to make room for other important daily tasks.

Another unexpected benefit is that time tracking helps you discover your own “rhythm.” Whether you are aware of it or not, most of us have surprisingly rigid daily patterns. Unfortunately, these patterns cannot be easily reshaped at will, so the next best way to improve daily performance is to align your schedule with your natural rhythm. This insight naturally leads into the scheduling aspect of time management.

Once you have enough data on how you naturally spend your time, you can start planning how to use each waking hour more productively. As you may realize the first time you write it down, planning itself takes time. You can use tools with recurring features to [automate](/2026/04/06/automation/) parts of the process, but it still requires effort. And once you start following your own schedule, you will quickly discover that you cannot follow it perfectly—and that is okay.

The main benefit of planning is not that everything will go as envisioned, which is impossible, but that it helps you make quicker and better decisions when transitioning between tasks. When you have an overview of the entire day and have already considered possible outcomes, you can prioritize tasks in a way that does not sacrifice your overall productivity. That is how scheduling improves efficiency.

As a heads-up, there are a few tips that can help you get started more smoothly. When it comes to time tracking, most people—including myself—find digital tracking to require the least effort. There are many tracking apps available, but most of them use the Toggl API in the background, so using Toggl directly may be the simplest option. When tracking, you do not need to specify every single task, as that creates unnecessary friction. Instead, you can group activities into broad categories such as “Chores,” “Entertainment,” “Main Work,” or “Studying.”

The same idea applies to time scheduling. You do not need to plan every small task down to the minute; using 15–60 minute blocks allows for faster planning and a better grasp of the overall day. You can plan each day individually or plan your entire week at once—it depends on how predictable your schedule is, and you should adapt accordingly. That is precisely why both time tracking and time scheduling are essential components of effective time management.
