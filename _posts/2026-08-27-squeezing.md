Even when you have gone through a workflow multiple times, it is still hard to avoid Hofstadter's Law: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." This becomes quickly noticeable when you do hyper-scheduling and [time tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/) every day.

Here is my experience as an example. The last time I did the task, it took about two hours, so I allocated two-hour time blocks for every time I would repeat it the next day. When the day came, I realized I had failed to account for the difference in quantity within the same task, so it took three hours instead of two to finish the first round. Adding one hour to each round, I ended up going home three hours later than planned.

Such [failures](/2026/08/18/learning-from-failures/) would incentivize anyone to not cram too much work into their schedules and start adding margins to account for major miscalculations. However, now we have to deal with a second problem: Parkinson's Law: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

The more time we have, the slower we act. The quality-to-speed ratio won't change much for work that we already enjoy, but for tasks that we don't, we start procrastinating more while accomplishing them with less than half of our usual attention. In addition, we become less motivated to improve our efficiency. To use the earlier example, when I became fed up with how long the task was taking, I reversed some of the steps to see whether I could do it faster. Surprisingly enough, it worked, allowing me to finish more work in the same two hours.

There is an obvious [conflict](/2026/09/13/system-conflict/). We don't want to squeeze too many tasks into our schedules and ruin our afternoon and evening plans the next day. At the same time, we don't want to reduce our productivity per hour either.

The working solution for now is to always contain a hidden one-hour margin in the schedule, but pretend that it doesn't exist during planning. To make it specific, if your shift normally ends at 6 p.m., schedule as if you plan to go home at 5 p.m., and add an extra 10 minutes to the non-work-related blocks afterward, such as commuting, reading, and eating. Of course, you will fail to follow it to the T most of the time, since discovering time hacks is rare. Still, because we like to stick to our promises, we try to focus on the task at hand to finish it "on time." And even if you are an hour behind schedule, your night plans won't be messed up along with it because you can make up for the delay by using those hidden pockets of excess time sprinkled across multiple blocks.

Time management, even on a 24-hour scale, is tricky because how you plan affects how you execute. To borrow the words of Eisenhower: "Plans are worthless, but planning is everything," plans are, at the end of the day, just another productivity tool. So if the conventional scheduling method is failing, add some secret features. We don't have to trade rigidity for flexibility, or vice versa. Both can be baked into the system, ready to be activated at the right moment for the best performance.
