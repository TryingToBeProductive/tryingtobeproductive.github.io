*Estimated Reading Time : 4min*

When we read headlines, everything seems to happen suddenly or to have only been developing for a few weeks. Of course, if you dive deeper into any [news](/2026/03/02/news/), statements, or changes, you would quickly realize that the seeds were planted a long time ago and only managed to get our attention now.

Even at the individual level, mastering a skill, having a eureka moment, or completing any project takes a frustratingly long time. Furthermore, when we look at legends in a specific field, our daily efforts pale in comparison to theirs. This extreme commitment can be summed up by Srinivasa Ramanujan's quote, "It came to me in a dream." He spent every waking hour thinking about and doing mathematics, to the point that he started solving some of the hardest problems even while asleep.

As the saying goes, we can't rush perfection. We have to take time to achieve greatness, or so it implies. However, we also know what happens when we allow ourselves to take things slowly: we procrastinate.

Sadly, our biology prioritizes laziness to save energy. As such, it would take any chance to slack off, especially when we feel resistance from the work. Some people have effectively utilized the time spent wandering off to come up with creative solutions. But most of the time, it simply ends up with us being sluggish.

When possible, we would like to take time to hone our craft, execute with quality, and think through our plans in detail without becoming unreasonably slow. Simply allocating the right amount of time isn't enough; it has to be justified.

When we are in [manager](/2026/03/28/manager-and-worker/) mode, we view 24 hours as a budget to be spent. We give an hour here and a few hours there so that, when we are in worker mode, we can accomplish our given tasks properly. However, we sometimes waste that time due to incompetence or a lack of pressure. Yet, when there is no report on how well the time was used, we start to misinterpret our [time tracking](/2026/02/09/time-management/) results as "It took more time than expected" rather than "My own generosity caused a drop in my performance."

Therefore, before your manager-self makes more time for your worker-self, ask whether the amount of time used previously was justified. Since you are simply talking to yourself through different mindsets, you can be honest. If your worker-self states that they didn't get a [break](/2026/02/26/taking-breaks/) for a single second last time, easing the time constraints is recommended. On the other hand, if your worker-self admits that they weren't being as productive as they should have been, decreasing the amount of work time may improve their performance.

We learn time and time again that everything takes time. However, allowing yourself to slow down doesn't necessarily mean that you will put more effort into your work. Similar to budgeting, resource usage has to be justified. When we talk about feedback, we tend to focus on how successful the method was or how close we are to the goal, but pay less attention to the cost behind the progress. Especially when the cap is fixed, wasted time can severely affect the progress of other tasks. Long-term commitment will create results, but we can make the road toward them smooth or bumpy. Making justification a requirement allows us to use time more effectively and be more productive without guilt.
