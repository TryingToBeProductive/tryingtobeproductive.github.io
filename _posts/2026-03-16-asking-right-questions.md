Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How. The famous 5W1H method for quickly performing a comprehensive analysis. You may also have heard of the reductionist approach: examining each small piece in order to understand the whole. In either case, finding a solution begins with asking questions. The recipient of our inquiry can be a person, a machine, or even ourselves. If we want to reach answers efficiently, we need to at least reach a level where we can ask the right questions. But how do we get there?

When you are in a familiar field, you immediately recognize that amateurs and experts ask questions differently. Even though beginners may sometimes notice problems or insights that are easier to see from an outsider’s perspective, most of the time veterans have already considered what outsiders think is important. Instead, they focus primarily on subtle [details](/2026/03/06/details/), real bottlenecks, or the seeds of potential breakthroughs. Thinking like an expert is difficult because it requires a significant amount of background knowledge. However, once enough context is accumulated, we can begin crossing out questions that are already answered or low [priority](/2026/03/05/priorities/) and start searching for answers to the truly important unknowns.

So if we want to ask the right questions, we first need to fill in our gaps in knowledge. Or in simpler terms: “shut up and learn.” Absorbing new material from scratch is definitely time-consuming. If you can delegate the work to professionals in exchange for giving up some control, it may be more economically sensible. But if it is your job—or if you need to maintain full control over your projects—you must accept short-term inefficiency in order to gain long-term efficiency. Fortunately, once the fundamentals are mastered, you will naturally become more capable of surgically isolating the core issue from the larger system.

Before analysis, there are inquiries. And before questioning, there is a period of study. Once the basic conditions are met, we begin asking fewer [foolish](/2026/05/17/silliness/) questions and more intelligent ones. Ideally, we want to spend more time solving problems rather than searching for them. By investing our resources early in developing the ability to identify the heart of an issue quickly, we can later receive the dividends of greater productivity.
